Zero to Ground: Hypersonics Learning Track

Practical advice to gain ground quickly with zero prior experience. Six sections, in order: lock the fundamentals, read the papers that are closest, use simple simulation tools, talk to people already doing the work, pick one small buildable piece, and track the energy and mass ledger every time.

This page is a study tracker. It does not fly anything, heat anything, or model a real vehicle. The shock visualizer is a teaching model, labeled as such. The measured numbers on this page come from wind-tunnel and expansion-tube tests, not from flight.

"Improving supersonic flights with femtosecond laser filamentation", Science Advances 2018, 4: eaau5239. This is drag work, not a heat shield.		 DRAG ONLY, NOT HEAT FLUX Drag, transient. Warning that keeps the laser lane honest: Alberti et al. (Univ. of Illinois CHESS), self-consistent CFD of supersonic drag reduction via upstream-focused laser-energy deposition: the hot spot distorts the bow shock and momentarily cuts drag, but the challenge of increased heat flux during certain phases and shock issues induced by pulses remains unresolved. Rezaei Sangtabi et al., Aerospace Science and Technology 76 (2018) 72-81: a single laser pulse can INCREASE drag; splitting the energy into smaller high-frequency pulses is what cuts it. 3. Simulation tools

Free or low-cost options: OpenFOAM (open-source CFD), or browser-based flow visualizers. Start by modeling a simple blunt body with and without a low-density region ahead of it. The goal is intuition, not perfect numbers.

Teaching model, not CFD. Stagnation-point heat flux uses Sutton-Graves: q = 1.7415e-4 * sqrt(rho / R) * V^3, q in W/cm2, rho in kg/m3, R nose radius in m, V in m/s. Assumptions stated plainly: rho is fixed at a teaching value of 0.02 kg/m3, and V = Mach * 340 m/s. The reduction curve is illustrative; the measured anchors are MEESST 40 to 83% heat-flux reduction in a wind tunnel, Gulhan 2009 with 46% (blunt) and 85% (flat) heat-flux reduction measured in argon in the L2K arc-heated tunnel, and Tokyo 16% thicker shock layer with no heat flux measured. Stand-off factor is illustrative and anchored: 1 + 0.10 * B gives 1.16x at 1.58 T, matching the Tokyo measured 16%.

4. Talk to people already doing the work

University groups working on hypersonics or HTS magnets (search MEESST partners, Tokyo Metro, or U.S. university hypersonics labs). Many will answer a short, specific email from someone who has read their paper. Conferences and online forums (NASASpaceflight, Reddit r/AerospaceEngineering) are lower-stakes places to ask questions.

5. Pick one small, buildable piece

Instead of the whole system, focus on one subsystem. Click one to track it. Document what you learn.

(a) Textured or channeled surface

Question: can a textured or channeled surface reduce local heating in a small plasma torch or arc-jet test?

What it would prove: whether surface texture changes local heating under a plasma jet. Access needed: university or makerspace plasma torch or arc-jet equipment.

(b) Simple coil and a laboratory plasma

Question: what does a simple coil do to a laboratory plasma?

What it would prove: whether a coil field visibly moves or shapes a lab plasma. Access needed: university or makerspace plasma equipment and a coil.

(c) Blunt body, low-density region ahead

Question: what changes when a blunt body flies with and without a low-density region ahead of it?

What it would prove: whether a low-density region ahead of the body moves the shock or changes heating. Access needed: simulation first (OpenFOAM or a flow visualizer), wind-tunnel time later.

6. Energy and mass ledger
