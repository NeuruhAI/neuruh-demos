The AI and SI Populace: A Storyboard for Ordinary People

Most people hear the words artificial intelligence and superintelligence and feel nothing close to home. It sounds distant, or threatening, or built for someone else. This page is the bridge across that gap. It gives Jeramie Hicks a way to talk with ordinary people, in plain language, so they can see the positive side and bloom into understanding. It is structure, not script. No page here tells him what to say word for word. Every section gives him hooks, one idea per beat, stories to reach for, transitions, time boxes, and what to close on. He fills it with his own words, in the room, in the moment.

1. How to use this page

Read it like a map before a drive, not like lines before a play. Know the route, then drive it your own way.

The one rule. One idea per beat, one story per idea. If a beat carries two ideas, split it. If an idea has no story, find one from your own life before you use it. People remember the story. The idea rides along inside it.

How a talk is built here. Pick the avatar in the room. Read that avatar's profile in section 4. Take its hook, its bridge, and its close. Fit them into one of the talk skeletons in section 5. That is the whole method. Nothing else on this page needs to be in the talk.

Time boxes. Every avatar row in the pain-point map carries a time box. That is the minutes that avatar's bridge needs, not a limit on the conversation. A short talk uses one avatar and one bridge. A long talk uses three beats and three bridges, with a transition between each one.

What this page never does. It never gives word-for-word lines to read aloud. It never talks down to anyone in the room. It never invents a fact, a number, a partner, or an outcome. Anything marked as an open question stays open until Jeramie confirms it. See section 9.

Reading level. The body copy here is written for an eighth or ninth grade reading level: short sentences, plain words, one idea at a time. Jargon appears only when it is defined in one line the first time it shows up.

The tone to hold: elegant, like listening to a great book. Slow enough to follow, plain enough to repeat, warm enough to trust. The listener should leave able to explain the idea to someone else at the kitchen table.

2. The PI sector, in plain language

Open question, stated first: what does PI mean? The estate does not settle this. No source in the Neuruh records defines the letters PI, and no source equates PI with Personal Intelligence. The phrase personal intelligence does exist in the estate, attached to two things: the Jeramie Neural Core compound architecture, and MIRROR-X1, described as a personal intelligence organ. Personal OS is a separate thing, a filesystem and registry structure, and it is never called Personal Intelligence in its own documents. The letters SI are also not verified as an abbreviation in the records, even though the framing document defines the end state as sovereign intelligence. Until Jeramie confirms the reading, PI on this page means the working read below, and nothing more. The three ideas that follow are that working read, clearly labeled as such.

Personal intelligence is intelligence that belongs to one person and works for that person. Not a system that watches you. A system that remembers for you, thinks with you, and helps you act on what you already know.

Three plain ideas carry the whole sector. A ninth grader should be able to repeat each one back after hearing it once.

Idea one: it remembers

Everyone carries more in their head than they can hold at once. Notes in five places, decisions made on the phone, reasons forgotten by Tuesday. Personal intelligence keeps what you know, what you decided, and why you decided it, in one place you can search. The story to reach for: a drawer full of paper that finally has labels on it. Jargon, defined: a receipt is a sealed record of what happened, who did it, when, and under what authority, so the work can be checked later.

Idea two: it thinks with you

It does not think instead of you. It lays out the options side by side, names what each one costs and what each one risks, and points at the next step. You still choose. The story to reach for: a second set of eyes on a contract, a budget, or a plan, the kind of friend who reads the fine print out loud. Jargon, defined: a tradeoff is what you give up when you pick one option over another.

Idea three: it helps you act

Knowing is not doing. Personal intelligence turns the decision into a move: the call to make, the form to file, the buyer to contact, the price to check. Then it keeps the receipt, so you can prove what you did and learn from how it went. The story to reach for: a checklist that checks itself off and remembers how it went last time.

What personal intelligence is not. It is not a robot that replaces you. It is not a company watching you. It is not a shortcut that skips the thinking. It is your own knowledge, organized, so you can use it when it counts. State label: the Jeramie Neural Core architecture behind this idea is Implemented, with a Live demo. MIRROR-X1 is a one-line product map entry only, with no build, spec, or demo on record. Personal OS is Designed, with its registry Blocked and its scanner unbuilt. See section 9.

How to read the table. Pain point is what hurts. Fragmented perception is what the person currently believes that is incomplete or wrong. Bridge is the one reframe that lands. Talking point is the single prompt to carry into the room. Time box is the minutes that bridge needs.

4. Custom avatar profiles

The heart of the page. One section per avatar. Each one gives a hook, a heartstring, a bridge, a close, and what not to say. The heartstring is the emotional pull, honestly earned, never manipulative: it names something true about the person's life and honors it. It never uses fear, guilt, or pity to move them.

Two rules that hold across every profile. First, the women's profiles seed through enhancement and natural beauty and comfort, never through chores. The forbidden pattern is named here so it is never used by accident: telling mothers to learn to cook better, or framing a woman's worth through housework she should do more of, is forbidden on this page and in any talk built from it. Second, the men's profiles never lean on hustle-bro or get-rich framing. No grind language, no promises of fast money, no framing a man's worth through how hard he chases a dollar.

5. Talk structures

Two skeletons. Both are bullet prompts only, never sentences to read aloud. Pick the avatar first, then fit that avatar's hook, bridge, and close into the skeleton.

The 3 to 5 minute talk skeleton

  • Minute 0 to 1, hook. Prompt: the avatar's hook from section 4, told as a story from your own life that matches it.
  • Minute 1 to 2, one idea. Prompt: one of the three plain ideas from section 2, the one that fits this avatar best. One idea only.
  • Minute 2 to 3, one story. Prompt: a story to reach for that shows the idea working in ordinary life. Your story, not a borrowed one.
  • Minute 3 to 4, one bridge. Prompt: the avatar's bridge from section 4, in your own words. Name the pain, name the reframe, stop there.
  • Minute 4 to 5, one ask. Prompt: what to close on. One small next step, not a sale. A question to try, a tool to open once, a memory to save, a decision to lay out.

What to close on: the listener leaves with one idea they can repeat at the kitchen table, and one small step they can take this week.

The 10 to 15 minute ramble skeleton

Same spine as the short talk, expanded to three beats, with a transition between each one. A ramble wanders on purpose. The spine keeps it from getting lost.

  • Minute 0 to 2, hook. Prompt: open with the avatar's hook, then widen it: why this room, why this person, why now.
  • Minute 2 to 5, beat one: it remembers. Prompt: the first plain idea, with a story from your own life. Transition prompt: from remembering to thinking, the question that connects them.
  • Minute 5 to 8, beat two: it thinks with you. Prompt: the second plain idea, with a second story, ideally from a different part of your life. Transition prompt: from thinking to acting, the moment a decision becomes a move.
  • Minute 8 to 12, beat three: it helps you act. Prompt: the third plain idea, with the avatar's bridge woven in. This is the longest beat, because this is where the room sees itself in the idea.
  • Minute 12 to 15, close. Prompt: return to the hook, name the three ideas in one breath, then the ask. One small next step, and an open door: the free ten-minute call at cal.com/neuruh for anyone holding a live deal or a real decision.

What to close on: the listener leaves able to repeat all three ideas, and with one small step and one open door. If the ramble runs long, cut a story, never the close.

The Small-Town Moonshot talk beat

A beat to insert into the ramble skeleton, or to stand alone at 5 to 7 minutes. Thesis status: this is Jeramie's thesis from the Tech Skills Shortage Thesis conversation, August 30, 2026, not established fact.

  • Minute 0 to 1, hook. Prompt: his 2004 graduation, the 135-student class, and the 35 who want to be welders for rockets and build tech for Mars.
  • Minute 1 to 3, the gap. Prompt: the thesis in one breath, America industrialized skilled labor but academicized skilled technology, and the AI buildout is exposing the gap. Prompt: the missing third pathway, the Technology Trades, and the eight roles it holds: network technician, controls programmer, data-center technician, robotics integrator, cybersecurity operator, industrial AI technician, compute infrastructure specialist, autonomous-systems technician.
  • Minute 3 to 5, the Moonshot. Prompt: one building, the tools inside it, who meets whom inside it, and the thing they build together. Prompt: the retired tool-and-die worker teaching, the engineer appearing remotely, the local manufacturer with a real problem.
  • Minute 5 to 7, the close. Prompt: the ownership layer and the lost-knowledge layer, then the close below, in your own words.

His close, from the conversation: "Don't train Middle America to consume the future. Give it the tools to build and own the future." And: "Stop watching the future happen somewhere else. Build it here." Both lines are Jeramie Hicks, Tech Skills Shortage Thesis conversation, August 30, 2026. They are quoted here as his thesis, not as lines to read aloud. Say them your own way in the room.

6. Grok focus: the tool he speaks from

Jeramie's words: Grok, hands down. He speaks from the tool he uses daily. That is personal testimony, not a spec sheet. This section stays inside that lane.

Why Grok is the focus. Because it is the tool in his hand every day. When he talks about what personal intelligence feels like, he is describing his own mornings with it: asking, checking, drafting, deciding. The audience trusts a tool its speaker actually uses. That is the whole case, and it is enough.

Verified facts only, and nothing else. Grok is xAI's AI assistant. It is available at grok.com, inside the X app, and as standalone iOS and Android apps. Voice mode exists. A free tier with limits exists. The standalone app and grok.com do not require an X account. No prices appear on this page, because third-party sources conflict after the October 2026 reshuffle. No model numbers, no invented specs, no invented capabilities.

Muse, acknowledged plainly. Muse is a capable tool, and Jeramie is not opposed to it. It is not the focus here, because Grok is the tool he reaches for daily. That is a statement about his habit, not a ranking of the tools. No comparison table appears on this page, because any table would have to invent numbers to fill its cells, and this page invents nothing.

How to talk about Grok in the room. Prompt: the moment in your own day when you open it, what you ask it first, and what it gives back that you keep.
