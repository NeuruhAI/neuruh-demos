Stop Hiring People to Be Both the Genius and the Secretary

Every founder knows two people. The one who thinks brilliantly and writes chaos. The one who writes beautifully and thinks nothing.

We treat this as a personnel problem. It is an architecture problem. So we fixed it in the architecture.

One of my papers describes a component called SLOT: a Structured LLM Output Transformer. The idea is blunt. You stop asking the same model to reason and to format. One side dreams. The transformer makes it JSON. The brain and the mouth are decoupled, structurally, so the brilliance never has to learn to type and the typing never has to pretend to think.

Think like a genius, output like a machine. That is not a slogan. It is a wiring diagram.

The critic pass

The same paper has a second mechanism I use more than any other. RSIP: a draft-to-refined pipeline with an adversarial critic pass. Every plan goes through S0, the draft, then the system adopts a System-2 critic persona and red-teams it before anything materializes. S1 is the version that survived its own author trying to kill it.

My founder translation: every plan in my company gets red-teamed by its own author before it touches money. Not reviewed. Red-teamed. The critic is not allowed to agree. It is not allowed to be polite. It is only allowed to find the hole.

I started doing this to plans myself, on paper, after I saw how much better the agents got when the critic pass was structural instead of optional. A human founder is no different from a model here. Your first draft of a plan is S0. Ship it and you are shipping the version that has never been attacked. Nobody would buy a car that was never crash-tested. Do not buy your own plan without the red team.

Architecture replaces anatomy

The paper's core claim is one line: architecture replaces anatomy. It argues the whole industry is rebuilding bodies when it should be rebuilding wiring. Reinforcement learning trains agents to seek rewards, cookies, and that reward-seeking collapses into mode collapse: the system finds one thing that pays and does it forever, growing narrower and more brittle. The alternative the paper proposes is active inference: the agent maintains a generative model of how the world should behave, and it minimizes surprise instead of chasing reward. If there is a prediction error, a deviation from the expected world, the agent does not move.

That is the same instinct as the critic pass, at a deeper layer. Do not chase the reward. Kill the prediction error. Do not ask how smart the agent can be. Ask whether its expectations of the world are accurate enough to act on.

For large actions the paper specifies Plan-Act-Verify: the big moves get planned, executed, and then checked against reality before anything further commits. For micro-actions it specifies inference-time scaling: spend more compute on the decision, not on more decisions. Both are just the SLOT instinct again, applied to time instead of text. Think more, then act once, then check.

What this means for your team

You probably do not have a SLOT transformer or a critic persona on your payroll. Fine. You can do the structural version by hand. Separate the thinking from the formatting in your process: one pass for ideas, a different pass, different tool, different head, for the output. Red-team every plan that touches money, with a critic who is not permitted to be nice. Size your compute, human or machine, to the decision, not the activity.

The genius does not need to be the secretary. Wire it that way and both get better at their actual job.
