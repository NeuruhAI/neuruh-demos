1. The cast

Aaron Reitz — U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of Texas (Houston). Appointed by President Trump; previously ran the DOJ Office of Legal Policy. Author of the November 11 preservation letter. Posted it on X, calling it "the first step to stop this abuse of power."

Sen. Richard Blumenthal (D-CT) — Ranking member, and incoming chair in the 120th Congress, of the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations. Former U.S. Attorney and Connecticut Attorney General (20 years). Author of the donor inquiry and of the November 11 reply.

Trent McCotter — Acting Deputy Attorney General, the second-highest-ranking DOJ official. Publicly backed Reitz: "The First Amendment expressly prohibits Congress from infringing the speech and association rights of Americans. DOJ will defend against threats to those rights, period."

Will Scharf — White House Counsel. Sent a November 9 letter to Rep. Robert Garcia warning that Democrats' planned oversight "may cross the line into blatant criminality."

Attorney General Todd Blanche — Asked November 12 about Reitz's letter; said "any office could investigate that." Told reporters November 12 (Thursday) that there are no open investigations into Democrats' rhetoric and that warning of looming investigations is not a crime.

Rep. Robert Garcia (D-CA) — Incoming chair of the House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform. Recipient of Scharf's November 9 letter; replied that Congress draws its investigative power from Article I, not from White House permission.

President Donald Trump — On November 7 called Democrats' threatened investigations "extortion" and said he was ordering DOJ to look into it. On November 12 called six Democratic lawmakers the "Seditious Six" and asked, "Will the Seditious Six ever be prosecuted?"

3. The statutes

Reitz's letter cites two sections of Chapter 13 of Title 18, the federal civil-rights chapter. They are different tools, and the difference matters. Section 241 is the conspiracy statute. Section 242 is a substantive deprivation statute that requires action under color of law, done willfully. Neither one is a charge here. Both are quoted below from the U.S. Code as published by govinfo (2022 edition).

18 U.S.C. § 241 — Conspiracy against rights The conspiracy statute

"If two or more persons conspire to injure, oppress, threaten, or intimidate any person in any State, Territory, Commonwealth, Possession, or District in the free exercise or enjoyment of any right or privilege secured to him by the Constitution or laws of the United States, or because of his having so exercised the same; or If two or more persons go in disguise on the highway, or on the premises of another, with intent to prevent or hinder his free exercise or enjoyment of any right or privilege so secured—"

Elements a prosecutor would have to prove:

  1. Two or more persons — a conspiracy needs at least two.
  2. An agreement — they conspired; a shared plan, not parallel conduct.
  3. Intent to injure, oppress, threaten, or intimidate.
  4. The target right — the victim's free exercise of a right secured by the Constitution or federal law, or retaliation for having exercised it.
  5. An overt act in furtherance of the conspiracy.
OutcomeMaximum penalty
Base offenseFine and/or up to 10 years
Death results, or kidnapping, aggravated sexual abuse, or attempt to killAny term of years or life, or death

History: traces to the Enforcement Act of 1870, passed so the federal government could prosecute the Klan for terrorizing formerly enslaved people. That origin is why the statute is written about conspiracy and intimidation, not about a single official's letter.

18 U.S.C. § 242 — Deprivation of rights under color of law NOT a conspiracy statute

"Whoever, under color of any law, statute, ordinance, regulation, or custom, willfully subjects any person in any State, Territory, Commonwealth, Possession, or District to the deprivation of any rights, privileges, or immunities secured or protected by the Constitution or laws of the United States, or to different punishments, pains, or penalties, on account of such person being an alien, or by reason of his color, or race, than are prescribed for the punishment of citizens, shall be fined under this title or imprisoned not more than one year, or both…"

Elements (Congressional Research Service, R43830, 2014):

  1. The victim was an inhabitant of a U.S. state, district, or territory when the alleged violation occurred.
  2. The defendant acted under color of law.
  3. The defendant's conduct deprived the victim of a right secured or protected by the U.S. Constitution.
  4. The defendant acted willfully — that is, with specific intent to violate the protected constitutional right.
OutcomeMaximum penalty
Base offenseFine and/or up to 1 year
Bodily injury, or use / attempted / threatened use of a dangerous weapon, explosives, or fireFine and/or up to 10 years
Death results, or kidnapping, aggravated sexual abuse, or attempt to killAny term of years or life, or death

The "willfully" element is the hard one. In Screws v. United States, the Supreme Court read § 242 to require specific intent to violate a federal right, not just a generalized bad purpose. Federal courts have acquitted where the evidence showed excessive force or intent to harm but not the specific intent to deprive someone of a constitutional right (United States v. Shaffer, cited in CRS R43830). A letter that asserts a legal theory, however aggressively, is a long way from that standard.

The distinction, stated plainly

§ 241 requires a conspiracy. One person acting alone cannot violate it. If the allegation is that Blumenthal alone sent letters, § 241 fails at element one unless a second conspirator and an agreement are identified. If the allegation is that Reitz alone sent a letter, the same problem applies in the other direction.

§ 242 requires action under color of law, done willfully. It is the statute historically used against state actors, typically law enforcement, who deprive someone of a constitutional right. It does not require a conspiracy, but it does require the specific intent to violate a known constitutional right. A Member of Congress conducting an inquiry, and a U.S. Attorney sending a preservation letter, both act under color of law in the broad sense — the fight is over whether either one's conduct actually deprived anyone of a right, and whether it was done willfully.

Neither statute criminalizes oversight, and neither criminalizes a preservation letter. What each one criminalizes is a specific act, done with a specific intent, against a specific right. That is what the bridge below tests, element by element.

6. Counter-arguments and defenses

The oversight defense (Blumenthal's side)

Congressional oversight is a legitimate legislative function. The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed Congress's power to investigate the executive branch for legitimate legislative purposes (McGrain v. Daugherty, 273 U.S. 135 (1927); Watkins v. United States, 354 U.S. 178 (1957); Trump v. Mazars, 591 U.S. 848 (2020)). Investigating whether donors received lawsuits dismissed, contracts awarded, or regulations unenforced in exchange for gifts is squarely within that zone: it is the subject of existing anti-bribery and ethics statutes. Congress has wide latitude to determine legislative purpose, and a subject already covered by statute falls squarely in it.

The Speech or Debate Clause (U.S. Const. art. I, § 6, cl. 1) protects Members' legislative acts from being questioned in any other place. Sending inquiry letters in preparation for legislation is a legislative act. A prosecutor's letter threatening "significant legal consequences" over that act is, on this view, the intimidation — not the inquiry.

Republicans used the same oversight power while holding Congress: investigations into President Biden, his family, and his Cabinet, including the failed impeachment of Homeland Security Secretary Alejandro Mayorkas and attempts against Defense Secretary Lloyd Austin and Attorney General Merrick Garland. (USA TODAY, Nov 15.)

The association defense (Reitz's side)

The First Amendment protects freedom of association, including the right to give to a cause and to associate with a President without government harassment. NAACP v. Alabama (1958) blocked a state from forcing a civil rights group to disclose its membership list — the case Reitz cites. On this view, a senator demanding donor records under threat of subpoena, aimed at people who gave to the President's project, is a government effort to punish association, and the donor letters are the threat.

The honest weakness in this argument, as the Above the Law account notes: NAACP v. Alabama protected a civil rights group's membership list from segregationists. Applying it to shield billionaire corporate donors from an inquiry into whether their gifts bought government favors is a stretch, and the donors in question are corporations with federal contracts and regulatory interests before the administration, not a private association.

The procedural facts that cut against both sides' criminal theories

A preservation letter is not a charge. Reitz's letter demands preservation and confirmation by November 18. It announces no indictment, no referral, and no finding. Blumenthal's reply is the same kind of document in reverse. Neither letter proves a crime; both preserve a record.

No charges have been filed against anyone. No court has ruled on any of this.

The DOJ itself says no open investigations exist. Attorney General Blanche told reporters November 12 that there are no open investigations into Democrats' rhetoric and that warning of looming investigations is not a crime. That statement sits awkwardly next to Reitz's letter, which is why Blumenthal's reply asks whether Blanche directed it.

The venue problem. A U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of Texas is writing to a Connecticut senator about an inquiry into a Washington project. That does not make the letter unlawful — Blanche said "any office could investigate that" — but it is the question every account of the exchange asks first, and it has not been answered on the record.
