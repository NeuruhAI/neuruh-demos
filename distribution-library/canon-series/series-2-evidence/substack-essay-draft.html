Series 2 — Evidence Precedes Confidence: Substack essay draft

Title: Evidence Precedes Confidence: The Day I Killed My Own Numbers

I have been building AI systems for two years. Most of what I built lived across a hundred repos, a Mac Mini, external drives, and a stack of internal canon documents nobody outside my head had ever read. What I told people about it, though, sounded like a company. I had valuations in my mouth: $6M indicative, $750K to $1.75M, $15-25M pre-money. 801 IP entries. 858 assets. 130 repositories I described as 130 products. Language like "quantum-proof" and "Palantir-class" that I would not let anyone check.

On June 30, 2026, I stopped letting myself get away with it.

I ran a code-verified reality audit. Adversarial stance, full access, one standard: does this claim trace to running code, passing tests, a deployed artifact, or a commit? If it didn't, it died.

Everything in the paragraph above died. Every legacy figure is now stamped SUPERSEDED, retained for history, never cited as current. It was, frankly, the most productive hour I have spent on the business in two years.

What survived is worth more than what died. The audit produced an evidence tier system that now governs every claim I make, and I am going to share the whole thing because I think every technical founder should steal it.

TIER A: Code-verified. Running code, passing tests, deployed artifact, or commit anchor. Claimable anywhere, including a data room.

TIER B: Built and inspectable. Exists, demonstrable, but not independently verified. Describable, never claimed as proof.

TIER C: Specced, scoped, or aspirational. A document. Documents don't execute. Never surfaced externally.

The Tier A inventory, June 30: 43 working codebases. 4 live. 1 deployed product engine. About 8 revenue-sellable product lines. 148 tests green on the scoring model. 49 signals. 43,897 records scored. 31 named IP frameworks.

And: $0 MRR. Tier A. The zero is in the same tier as the code, because the zero is what makes the code numbers credible. A founder who tells you his revenue is zero and his tests pass 148-for-148 is telling you something you can check. A founder who tells you he's worth $6M is telling you a story.

Out of this came the Valuation Honesty Rule: no dollar valuation is current or claimable before MRR exists. Not indicative. Not conservative. Not back-of-napkin. The conversation about value waits for revenue. Until then there is only evidence.

There is a cold commercial logic here, not just honesty-as-brand. An investor's diligence runs exactly the check I ran on myself. Publishing only what survives it turns you from a narrative risk into a verifiable counterparty. You become cheaper to diligence. That is worth more than any slide.

The hardest part of the audit was not the code review. It was deleting the sentences that made me sound impressive. Two years of building teaches you a lot about systems; it turns out it also teaches you how to inflate. Every founder has a set of those sentences. The discipline is finding yours before someone else does.

I have six more of these to write. Next: the autonomous factory, and the state machine that keeps the AI from shipping itself to production.
