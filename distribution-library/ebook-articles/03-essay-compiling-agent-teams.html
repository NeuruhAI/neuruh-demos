Subtitle: You already have the agents. Here is the practical playbook for compiling them into the smallest team that can do the job, designing receipts that prove the work, and turning every run into an asset that compounds.

How to Compile an Agent Team That Leaves Receipts

Most teams I talk to about agents have the same shape of problem. They have the orchestration framework. They have a pile of agents, tools, memory stores, and a governance dashboard that glows green. And they have one workflow that costs more than it should and nobody can explain why.

This essay is adapted from my book "The Org Chart Is the Product: Compiling Agent Teams That Leave Receipts" (part of The Neuruh Papers series). The book makes the strategic case. This is the how. How to take an objective, compile it into the smallest sufficient organization of agents, tools, models, memories, and human approvals, and keep the exact org chart that did the work as a record you can learn from. No new framework required. What follows is instrumentation and discipline, not invention.

Step one: decompose into evidence needs, not agent roles

The most common mistake in agent design is starting with roles. People name a research agent, a review agent, a synthesis agent, a debate coordinator. Roles are premature. They presuppose the org chart before you know the job.

Start with evidence needs instead. Ask: what facts must be established before this objective can be called complete? Evidence needs are falsifiable. You either got the tax record or you did not. You either verified the comparable sale or you did not. Roles are not falsifiable. "The research agent did research" tells you nothing.

Take a concrete example from the book: a property intelligence dossier. One parcel, one owner situation, a recommendation. The evidence needs are four: identity and ownership, encumbrance and tax status, valuation evidence, and synthesis. That is the whole decomposition. Everything after this step is mapping those needs to the cheapest capability that satisfies each one.

Map each evidence need to the cheapest capability that can satisfy it. Prefer deterministic tools over model inference, and retrieval over generation. A database lookup beats a reasoning chain when the fact is in the database. This sounds obvious. In practice, agent teams routinely send a model to reason about facts that a single query would return in milliseconds. Every one of those choices is cost and latency you can cut without touching quality.

Step two: compile the smallest sufficient topology

Now arrange the capabilities into the sparsest graph that respects the dependency order. The rule set is short:

  • Parallel where independent. The three evidence retrievals in the dossier example do not depend on each other. Run them in parallel.
  • Sequential where dependent. Synthesis depends on evidence. It goes after, not beside.
  • Human-gated where authority requires it. Anything that emits to a customer, spends money, or commits the business goes through a declared human approval before emission.

That is the supervisor-worker topology in its honest form. A supervisor decomposes the objective and assigns evidence needs. Workers execute in parallel and return. Synthesis runs in sequence. A human gate sits at exactly one edge: synthesis to emission. Not hovering over everything. One edge, declared, receipted.

Notice what is not in the topology. No debate. No all-to-all communication where every agent talks to every agent. Dense communication multiplies token cost and propagates noise: one agent's hallucinated confidence becomes another agent's cited fact becomes the final report's asserted finding. Sparse graphs are not just cheaper. At scale, they are more reliable, because noise has fewer paths to travel.

The selection criterion for every node and edge is sufficiency. The bias is toward less. Every component must earn its place by moving expected outcome quality or reducing cost, latency, risk, or unsupported claims. A component that does neither is decoration. You will ablate the decoration in step six.

Step three: put authority in the graph, not in the dashboard

This is the step most systems skip, and it is where production incidents are born. Every node in your topology carries a declared authority ceiling: observe, recommend, draft, stage, execute. An evidence-gathering worker holds observe and draft. It can read and prepare. It cannot emit. The synthesis pass holds draft and stage. The human approval node holds the single execute-capable edge to emission.

The mechanical rule is: what executes is the intersection of what the planner proposes, what policy permits, and what infrastructure can actually provide right now. Three sets. The planner proposes a topology. Policy permits a subset. Infrastructure provides a subset right now, because models go down, APIs rate-limit, and data goes stale. What runs is only what survives all three.

When an edge does not survive, record it. The planner wanted the paid enrichment vendor; policy says no paid enrichment without per-run human approval; the edge was reduced, the reason recorded, the rule cited, the fallback (the free county recorder path) noted. The absence of the edge is itself evidence. Six months later, when someone asks why the dossier lacked phone numbers, the answer is in the receipt: policy excluded it, here is the rule, here is the time.

Most governance systems govern the happy path and go quiet on the degraded path. But the degraded path is where the incidents live. A system that records its own reductions is a system you can trust when things go wrong, because things going wrong is when you need the record most.

Two boundaries. Self-approval is not governance. If the proposing agent is also the approving agent, you have one set, not three, and the intersection is theater. And a human on every step is not the claim either. Authority is a property of the graph, enforced mechanically, with human approvals as declared nodes at declared gates.

Step four: design receipts that actually prove work

A receipt is the org chart that did the work, kept as a first-class record, with every promotion, demotion, veto, cost, and outcome attached. Here is the schema. Seven fields.

  1. The exact organization. Which nodes existed, which edges were active, versioned against your registry so you can reconstruct the team six months later.
  2. The authority each part held. Declared ceilings per node. Which approvals were given, by whom, at what time, with what scope. A signed event, not a checkbox in a UI.
  3. What was reduced. Every proposed edge that did not survive the intersection, with the reason. Policy-blocked or unavailable, with the specific rule or fact cited.
  4. What changed during execution. Round-level deltas: edges added or removed, who proposed the change, what outcome followed. Not just "edge removed." Why it changed.
  5. The cost. Tokens, model calls, tool calls, wall time, attributed per node. Cost without attribution is accounting. Cost attributed to the topology is management information.
  6. The outcome. Measured against the objective, including a count of unsupported claims: assertions the system made that its evidence cannot back. Counting them per run is how you minimize them.
  7. A slot for late-arriving economic reconciliation. The dossier completed in minutes. The knowledge that the client used it in a live negotiation arrived days later. The receipt must accept the late truth, or you learn from completion metrics and become efficiently useless.

The rule that separates a receipt from a souvenir: it must carry semantic evidence, not just signatures. Why the route changed, which policy acted, which model proposed the change, what outcome followed. If your receipt layer cannot answer "why did the topology change in round four," it is incomplete. Go back and fill the gaps.

Define "consequential" before you instrument. Any run that emits an artifact to a human or another system, spends above a cost threshold, touches a production data store, or acts above observe authority gets a full receipt. Everything else gets a light receipt: run ID, topology hash, outcome, cost. Coverage of consequences, not paperwork for its own sake.

Step five: turn receipts into outcome memory

Outcome memory is the calibrated record of which topologies produced which outcomes at which cost under which authority. Not traces. Not logs. Memory implies organization and retrieval for a purpose: the next compilation. When you compile a topology for the next objective, you estimate expected outcome quality by querying memory. The estimate is only as good as the memory. The memory is only as good as the receipts. The receipts are only as good as the schema and the honesty behind it. The chain is unbroken or it is broken. There is no partial credit in provenance.

Three things compound.

First, topology-outcome pairs. Every run adds one: this organization, this cost, this outcome. After hundreds of runs you can see which configurations reliably create value and which reliably waste money. That is the entire game.

Second, calibration. Early estimates are guesses. Predicted versus actual reconciles over time, and the compiler's estimates tighten. A well-calibrated compiler can take on riskier topologies when the upside justifies it, because it knows the odds.

Third, transfer. The compilation discipline, the governance intersection, the receipt schema, and the review loop transfer across domains. The domain content does not. Each new domain starts with the machinery and an empty memory, and the memory fills from that domain's receipts. As of this writing, cross-domain transfer of the full protocol remains a proposed hypothesis from the July 2026 working paper, not a validated result. The honest version: bring the machinery, start the memory empty, earn the history.

One guardrail the book is explicit about: observation does not auto-promote. Receipts feed a review process. The review process has a human or an independent verifier in it. Proposed changes run in quarantine, shadow topologies against historical receipts, red-team tests, and promote only through a gate with rollback available. Letting the system rewrite its own topology from its own unreviewed outputs is a self-evaluation loop, and it will reinforce persuasive, wrong behavior.

Step six: ablate everything

Ablation is removing things on purpose to see what breaks. It is how the honesty rule "if removing a component changes nothing, it was decoration" becomes engineering instead of slogan.

Pick your highest-volume workflow. List every component. For each one, run the workflow with the component removed or replaced by the cheapest alternative: a deterministic lookup instead of a model call, a single pass instead of a debate, no retrieval instead of retrieval. Measure outcome quality, cost, latency, and unsupported claims. Kill what does not move the needle.

Three targets recur across systems. First, the debate. Replace the debating agents with one reasoning pass over the same evidence. If quality holds, the debate was theater. Second, the retrieval habit. Retrieval on every step is often a latency and cost tax on steps where the context already held the facts. Worse, each retrieval is a chance to inject a confident irrelevance. Third, the reviewer agent. Measure its intervention rate. A reviewer that has never blocked or materially changed an output in a hundred runs is not governance. It is latency wearing an authority costume. Either give it real blocking power with real criteria, or remove it and put the authority in the graph reduction where it belongs.

Then run the failure-injection version. Disable one dependency in staging: a model, a tool, a database, a route. Watch what the topology does. If it silently proceeds without the verification step, you found a governance hole. If it halts with no receipt, you found an observability hole. If it rewires, records the reduction, and completes with a flagged caution, you have a system worth trusting.

Step seven: run the monthly review

Schedule it now. The people who can kill configurations are in the room. The review has three outputs: configurations promoted (with evidence, through the gate), configurations killed (with the receipts that convicted them), and schema changes (with versioning). No fourth output. The review is not a brainstorm. It is a court, and the receipts are the exhibits.

Promotions between status labels happen only on evidence. A topology that ran two hundred times with measured outcomes moves from live to proven for the specific claim the data supports. Nothing moves on narrative. The labels are the rungs. The receipts are the evidence. The review is the gate.

The 90-day start

You do not need a new organism to start compounding. You need the receipt schema, one consequential workflow, and the monthly review. Freeze the schema in weeks one and two. Instrument the intersection in weeks three to six. Run the review loop in weeks seven to ten. Close one economic loop in weeks eleven and twelve: reconcile predictions against actual revenue, cost, risk, latency, and operator acceptance on one workflow, with a documented counterfactual. At day ninety you should be able to answer five questions: how many consequential runs produced full receipts, what your reduction rate is, which configurations the review killed and why, what your unsupported-claim rate is per run, and whether one workflow has a closed economic loop. If you can answer those five, you have a receipt layer.

What not to build: a second organism. Instrument what you have. Harden its contracts. Close its outcomes. The temptation to rebuild from scratch is architecture theater with a budget. And do not compress the semantics into signatures prematurely. Start with readable receipts. Compress when the volume demands it and the semantics are stable. A receipt nobody can read is a souvenir with better engineering.

The company that learns which organizations work, fastest, wins. The agents are cheap. The knowledge of how to deploy them is the asset. Start this quarter.
