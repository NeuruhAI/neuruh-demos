I Wrote an 11-Page Paper on My Own Architecture. Its Most Important Table Says What I Did Not Invent.

In July I formalized the core protocol of my AI organism into a position paper. N-Cell: a compact way to represent a running team of agents as bytes. Routing masks, authority masks, memory masks, confidence and risk scores, all packed into a 64-bit header. Governance expressed as a graph reduction: what the planner proposes, intersected with what policy permits, intersected with what infrastructure can actually provide. Every topology change leaves a receipt.

It is the most rigorous thing I have written about my own system. And the part I am proudest of is the table where I list what I did not invent.

The prior-art table

The paper has a full prior-art review. Microsoft's AutoGen and Magentic-One. OpenAI's Agents SDK. LangGraph. Anthropic's agent patterns and MCP. The Linux Foundation's agent-to-agent protocol. Palantir's AIP. ServiceNow's control tower. Salesforce Agentforce. Microsoft Copilot Studio. The AIOS research line. Then the research frontier: DyLAN, DyTopo, AdaptOrch, MetaGen, RAPS, A-Mem, Swarm Skills.

Then there is a table called "Defensible Novelty Boundaries," and it reads like a confession:

Eight-neighbor lattice and bitmask routing: established prior art. "Do not claim as invention."

Dynamic agent-team topology: established and rapidly advancing. Use as foundation.

Agent operating system: established research category. "Do not claim category ownership."

I wrote those rows about my own work, on purpose, in a paper carrying my name.

Why the honesty is the strategy

Here is the thing about this industry right now: everyone is claiming everything. Every startup with a LangGraph wrapper is "building AGI." Every demo with two agents talking to each other is "a swarm." The signal-to-noise ratio in AI architecture writing is roughly zero, and every founder in the space knows it, including the ones doing the claiming.

So the honest paper is a competitive weapon. When I sit across from a technical buyer or a serious researcher, I do not need them to believe I invented graphs. I need them to believe one specific thing: that when my system says something happened, it actually happened, and I can prove it. The moat is not the agent. The moat is the receipt of the exact computational organization that produced the outcome, tied to the evidence, the authority, the cost, and what it earned.

That is the row in the novelty table marked "potentially strong claim area": topology receipts tied to evidence, authority, cost, and economic outcome. Not the bytes. The receipts.

Eight hypotheses, zero hand-waving

The paper ends with eight testable hypotheses, each with the evidence required to promote it. Task-adaptive topology versus fixed topology, with confidence intervals. Governance graph reduction measured by red-team precision and recall. Sparse routing measured against dense routing on tokens and latency. Cross-domain transfer measured by code diff. And a minimum benchmark scorecard with thresholds like "zero silent high-risk execution" before any claim advances.

There is also a section on risks and failure modes that most papers would never include: architecture theater ("elegant names and diagrams can outrun running code and closed outcomes"), registry inflation ("built, active, deployed, tested, and commercially validated must remain separate statuses"), patent overreach, and AGI branding risk ("claims above demonstrated performance damage credibility").

I included the failure modes because I have lived them. My estate has real systems in all four of those states, and I have learned the hard way that calling something "done" when it is merely "implemented" is how you go broke with a beautiful architecture diagram.

The discipline

The paper's executive ruling is one paragraph, and I want every founder building agents to read it:

"The Neuruh architecture is real enough to formalize and test, but not yet proven enough to market as AGI. The strongest defensible claim today is a governed, dynamically composed agentic operating organism that converts opportunity signals into evidence-backed execution topologies and learns from measured outcomes."

That is the whole discipline. Say what is real. Say what is not yet proven. Say exactly what evidence would change the classification. Then go produce the evidence.

The industry runs on vibes and valuations. I am betting there is a market for the opposite: the founder who shows you the table of what he did not invent, and then shows you the receipts for what he did.

If you are building in this space, try it. Write the prior-art section about your own product first. List every piece you borrowed. Watch how much stronger the remaining claims get when they are the only ones left standing.
