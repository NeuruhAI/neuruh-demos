1. The thesis: water sorts, the weak die, the heavies stay

Placer concentration is hydraulic sorting plus differential survival. Water runs the same algorithm on every rock on Earth, so the output structure is always identical. Only the trace inputs (source rock) change, and that is the sand color.

  1. Chemical weathering kills the weak minerals first. Feldspar and mica, most of the volume of granite and gneiss, hydrolyze into clay. The clay layer is not a separate deposit. It is the corpse of the parent rock.
  2. Only the resistates survive transport. Quartz (Mohs 7, chemically inert), chert and flint, garnet (6.5 to 7.5, dense), zircon, tourmaline, rutile. Geologists measure sediment maturity by counting these survivors: the ZTR index (zircon, tourmaline, rutile). Given enough cycles, every sediment converges to the same end state: quartz sand plus clay plus durable gravel plus heavy resistates.
  3. Water sorts the survivors by settling velocity, a function of density and size. Quartz is about 2.65 specific gravity, garnet 3.5 to 4.3, magnetite 5.2. Wherever current velocity drops, inside bends, behind boulders, gradient breaks, the heavies drop out together and concentrate.
  4. Clay is the false bedrock. Heavies work down through loose gravel until they hit the impermeable clay lens and stop. They accumulate on top of the clay contact. Placer miners dig to clay for exactly this reason. Sand and clay composite near water is textbook trap stratigraphy.
  5. Chert and flint nodules are lag survivors. They form inside limestone, the limestone dissolves away in weak carbonic acid, and the insoluble nodules ride into the gravels intact because nothing in a streambed can degrade them.

Southwest Michigan is glacial outwash: the entire landscape is pre-sorted sediment. The ice sheet ground up Canadian Shield garnet-bearing gneiss and schist plus Michigan Basin cherty limestone, and meltwater ran the density sort at continental scale. Every creek cut is re-sorting already-sorted material.

The premise crack, named before the hunt: "sell critical minerals from a gravel pit" fuses two different businesses. Gravel pits sell aggregate, a cents-per-ton volume game. Critical minerals come from heavy mineral sands, paleo dune, beach, or river deposits enriched in high-density minerals. Same physics, different geologic address. Great Lakes glacial sediment runs about 1 to 2% by weight magnetite equivalent, hobby-panning territory, sub-economic to mine standalone. Garnet is the one that can clear at small scale: dense, abrasive-market, no chemical plant, no license.

2. The economics gate: revenue per ton

Revenue / ton_feed = Grade(%) × Recovery(%) × Price($/ton_conc)

Grade and recovery are entered as percentages. The gate compares modeled revenue per ton of feed against your all-in cost per ton of feed. Pass means the modeled revenue clears the modeled cost. It does not mean a real pit makes money.

Worked example: garnet at 2% grade, 70% recovery, $300/ton concentrate gives 0.02 × 0.70 × $300 = $4.20 per ton of feed. Against a $3.00/ton cost that passes by $1.20. At 1% grade the same setup gives $2.10 and fails. That is the whole gate: grade is the lever, and glacial grades are thin.

What clears, per the dossier: garnet needs about 1 to 3% grade at $200 to $400/ton. Ilmenite needs about 2% or more at $200 to $300/ton, marginal at glacial grades. Rutile and zircon are rare in glacial sediment and belong to coastal heavy-mineral sands only. Monazite (rare earths) is excluded regardless of price: thorium means NORM and NRC / state radiation licensing.

Monazite rule, hard exclusion: score for it, never handle it. A monazite flag is information that can be sold or joint-ventured. It is not inventory to touch. The scorer weights monazite / rare earths at 0.0 by design. It also collides with the existing ITAR/EAR posture on edge-agent: a radiological materials file open next to an export-control file is not wanted.

4. The halo explainer: what counts as a signal

The scorer pulls the USGS MRDS national database, isolates sand, gravel, and sediment pits, and scores each on provenance proximity to documented heavy-mineral occurrences. MRDS records a pit's existence but is about 88% null on what is actually in it, so stated content cannot be the signal. Proximity to source rock can be, because short glacial transport preserves grade.

  • 40 km halo: a radius around each documented heavy-mineral occurrence. A pit inside the halo carries a signal; a pit outside it does not. 154 of 1,707 pits fall inside a halo.
  • Weighting: inverse-distance weighted sum, sum of weight / (1 + distance/10), plus best mineral times 2.0. Closer occurrences count more.
  • HM vocabulary: 16 text keys (garnet, titan, ilmenit, rutile, zircon, monazit, rare earth, magnetit, staurolit, kyanite, heavy mineral, placer, ruby, sapphire, diamond, gold) matched against 12 MRDS text columns.
  • Economic weights: Garnet / Rutile / Zircon / HMS 3.0; Titanium / Ilmenite / Placer 2.5; Gold / Diamond 2.0; Magnetite / Staurolite / Kyanite 1.5; Corundum 1.0; Monazite / REE 0.0 by design.
  • Own-hit multiplier: a pit documenting its own heavies gets times 4.0. No pit in the output does: own_hm is "-" on all 1,707 rows, so the multiplier never fires.
  • Multipliers: status (Producer 1.25, Past Producer 1.15, Plant 1.00, Prospect 0.90, Unknown 0.70); MRDS record quality grade (A 1.30, B 1.20, C 1.05, D 1.00, E 0.90).
  • Play routing: Producer goes to OFFTAKE, Past Producer goes to TAILINGS, everything else goes to RECON.
  • Home base: Benton Harbor, 42.1167, -86.4542, haversine miles column.
