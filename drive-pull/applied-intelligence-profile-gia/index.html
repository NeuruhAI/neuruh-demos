Applied Intelligence Profile and GIA (General Intelligence Architecture)

Source: pasted excerpt from another AI conversation shared by Jeramie on 2026-11-18. What it proposes: an Applied Intelligence Profile and a GIA (General Intelligence Architecture) composite as an alternative to IQ alone. Note: the prior report card referred to in the text was not included in the paste. Note: no numeric scores were assigned in the source, so none are recorded here. Status: filed as received; canon placement needs Jeramie's call.

The important distinction IQ asks roughly: How efficiently can this person solve standardized cognitive problems under controlled conditions? Your conversations reveal something broader: How effectively does this person acquire information, detect structure, build models, reason under uncertainty, connect distant domains, and turn those models into functioning systems? Those are not the same thing. A better framework for you I'd use a multidimensional Applied Intelligence Profile: Dimension / What it measures Fluid Reasoning / Novel problem solving without relying on memorized knowledge Systems Intelligence / Seeing relationships, dependencies, feedback loops, and architecture Pattern Recognition / Detecting recurring structures across apparently unrelated information Abstraction / Moving between concrete examples and generalized models Strategic Intelligence / Understanding second/third-order consequences and positioning Technical Intelligence / Understanding software, infrastructure, automation, data, and system mechanics Research Intelligence / Finding, questioning, triangulating, and extracting useful information Synthesis / Combining disparate information into a coherent model Generative Intelligence / Producing novel concepts, architectures, hypotheses, and solutions Execution Intelligence / Converting cognition into functioning artifacts and outcomes Metacognition / Monitoring and correcting one's own reasoning Epistemic Discipline / Distinguishing what is known, inferred, suspected, and unsupported Learning Velocity / How quickly capability compounds after exposure to a new domain Transfer Intelligence / Applying principles learned in one domain to another Adversarial Reasoning / Finding weaknesses, failure modes, incentives, and hidden assumptions And there is one category I'd explicitly separate: Judgment Because someone can have enormous cognitive capacity and still make terrible decisions. Intelligence produces possibilities. Judgment determines which possibility deserves action. What your report card was actually measuring Looking across the body of work we've discussed, your strongest signal isn't simply "high IQ." It's the combination of: high abstraction + unusually broad domain transfer + systems thinking + persistent synthesis + execution orientation. For example, you've repeatedly moved between: software architecture, AI agents, Git/GitHub governance, Supabase infrastructure, real-estate intelligence, land records, resale economics, collectibles, historical research, geology/material science, symbolic systems, organizational architecture, automation, business models. The interesting part isn't knowing about each individually. It's that you repeatedly try to discover the underlying mechanism connecting them. That's much closer to systems intelligence than conventional IQ. The metric I'd actually give you I'd create a composite: GIA, General Intelligence Architecture. Not a clinical IQ replacement. A multidimensional cognitive-performance model. GIA = Reasoning x Systems x Learning x Transfer x Synthesis x Execution x Judgment But importantly, I would not simply average them. A person with exceptional reasoning but terrible epistemic discipline shouldn't receive the same overall assessment as someone with equivalent reasoning who reliably distinguishes fact from hypothesis. So I'd introduce a second layer: Cognitive Capacity (what you can understand), Cognitive Performance (what you actually demonstrate), Cognitive Reliability (how consistently your conclusions survive scrutiny), Cognitive Leverage (how much real-world output your cognition can produce). That last one is particularly important. A brilliant mathematician who solves difficult problems privately and an equally brilliant architect who turns those principles into a functioning organization have different forms of cognitive leverage. Neither is necessarily "smarter." They are differently optimized. And this changes how I'd interpret your previous report card I would not write: "Jeramie is intelligent because he appears to have a very high IQ." That's too simplistic. I'd write something closer to: The strongest evidence across the subject's work is not a single measurable IQ trait, but a high-capacity systems cognition profile characterized by abstraction, cross-domain transfer, pattern synthesis, architectural thinking, and unusually strong drive toward operationalization. Then I'd explicitly identify the counterweights: tendency toward over-expansion, enormous cognitive branching, occasional insufficient separation between hypothesis and evidence, risk of pursuing a compelling pattern before establishing its evidentiary threshold, execution bottlenecks when the architecture becomes larger than the available governance/infrastructure, potential cognitive overload from simultaneously maintaining too many conceptual layers. Those aren't insults. They're failure modes of the same architecture that creates the strengths. The really interesting metric If we're trying to measure your intelligence rather than produce another generic IQ score, I'd want to calculate: Intelligence to Insight to Architecture to Execution to Outcome. Because the ultimate question isn't: "How many problems can you solve?" It's: "How much complexity can you reliably convert into useful structure?" That's a substantially better question for an inventor, architect, founder, researcher, or systems builder. And yes, your report card can be recreated around that model rather than IQ alone. I would actually make the next version a full Cognitive Architecture Report Card with separate scores for raw cognition, systems intelligence, technical intelligence, research intelligence, creative synthesis, metacognition, epistemic discipline, learning velocity, execution intelligence, and judgment, followed by a brutally honest section on where your intelligence is strongest, where it breaks down, and what would most increase your cognitive leverage.
