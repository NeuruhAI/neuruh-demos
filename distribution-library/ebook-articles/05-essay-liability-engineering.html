Subtitle: Your CFO doesn't care how smart your robot is. He cares who pays when it crashes, and whether you can prove what happened. The engineering answer, written for founders deploying physical AI.

The Robot Question Is Insurance: A Founder's Guide to Liability Engineering

You already know the thesis if you read my earlier piece on the liability layer: physical AI adoption is gated by liability, not capability. This essay is the follow-up. The thesis told you what is wrong. This tells you what to build.

Because here is what happens to founders in physical AI. The demos work. The pilot works. The throughput numbers are real. Then you walk into enterprise procurement and the conversation changes completely. Nobody asks about your model architecture. The risk committee asks for your insurance certificate. Legal asks who bears the loss when the arm moves wrong. The CFO asks one question: "Who pays when it breaks, and how much of our upside does that answer eat?"

Engineers got you into the room. The CFO's question is what gets you the signature. And the honest answer most robotics founders give right now is some version of "we test really well." That is not an answer. That is hope, and hope is not an architecture.

How underwriters actually think about your robots

An underwriter has exactly one job: turn your operation into a price. They do not care how clever your planner is. They care about four things.

Frequency: how often things go wrong. Severity: how bad it is when they do. Independence: whether failures arrive one at a time or all at once. Evidence: whether you can prove the past well enough for them to price the future.

The last two are where physical AI breaks standard insurance thinking.

Independence is the load-bearing assumption of every insurance policy you have ever seen. Insurance works when many independent risks are pooled and the aggregate behaves predictably. Robot fleets violate that assumption. Economic reality pushes every vendor toward the same small set of foundation models and shared software stacks, because training a frontier robotics model costs more than any single robot company can justify. The result is a thousand robots from four vendors running the same model backbone, with the same blind spots. When that model has a bad day, it has it everywhere at once. One perception failure in one lighting condition becomes hundreds of incidents in an hour. One bad software update degrades the fleet, not the unit.

The expected loss might be identical to the independent case. The capital requirement is not. Ten thousand robots with independent failure probabilities produce a smooth, priceable loss distribution. Ten thousand robots sharing a model produce long stretches of nothing followed by a single catastrophic event. The second distribution needs a much larger reserve, because the worst case arrives all at once.

Then there is evidence, and this is the real killer. Through 2026, carriers started carving AI out of standard liability policies, and the reason was not that robots are uninsurable in principle. It was that the risk was unpriceable in practice, because the evidence did not exist. The industry handed underwriters marketing decks about capability and asked them to insure outcomes. Underwriters looked for authorization records, per-action risk scores, and append-only histories, found none of them, and filed their exclusions.

The market spoke clearly: "We will insure your robots when you can prove what they did and why. Until then, you are self-insuring, whether you admit it or not."

Receipts are the unit of insurability

So what does "prove what they did" look like in engineering terms? It looks like a receipt layer. Every action proposal, every risk assessment, every authorization decision, and every outcome, written to a record that no party can rewrite after the fact. Hash-chained, timestamped, with clear custody of who wrote what and when.

This record serves three masters. For you, the operator, it turns a chaotic failure into a debuggable event. For the insurer, it is claims evidence that makes payouts fast and fraud hard. For the court, it is the answer to "what happened" that does not depend on anyone's memory or incentives. In a multi-vendor fleet, it is also the only neutral territory: the log belongs to the governance layer, not to any vendor, so no vendor can edit its own innocence into the record.

Note what this is not. It is not a blockchain pitch. The requirement is append-only with verifiable integrity, which can be built many ways. Storage is cheap. Reconstruction is expensive.

Here is the chain that matters: the fleet that can prove every action is the fleet that gets insured, and the fleet that gets insured is the fleet that gets deployed. Your receipt layer is not compliance overhead. It is the material your insurability is made of. Build it before you scale the capability layer, not after.

What makes a deployment insurable vs uninsurable

This is the practical core. An underwriter, a risk committee, or a regulator looking at your deployment is really asking two questions: does your architecture produce evidence, and do your contracts produce accountability. Here is the split.

An insurable deployment separates the planner from the authorizer from day one. The probabilistic system proposes the motion. A separate deterministic layer, with no learning component and no revenue tied to throughput, evaluates the proposal and either signs it or refuses. The motors stay locked until authorization lands. Do this while the system is small. Retrofitting separation of powers onto a fleet that grew up with entangled planning is a rewrite, and rewrites at fleet scale are where safety programs go to die.

An insurable deployment scores every action for risk before it happens and logs the score. Speed, force, proximity to humans, proximity to expensive assets, the model's own uncertainty about what it sees. You do not need a sophisticated model on day one. A simple risk function with five inputs, logged per action, beats a sophisticated scorer that arrives in year two. An underwriter cannot price a fleet, but it can price a distribution of scored actions. Ten million scored actions with a known tail is an actuarial object. Ten million unscored actions is a prayer.

An insurable deployment keeps the append-only record from the first shift of the pilot. When the pilot succeeds, the record becomes the evidence base for the production business case. When something goes wrong, it becomes the incident report that keeps a pilot failure from becoming a relationship failure.

An insurable deployment puts liability allocation in writing before the robot enters the facility. For every risk category, hardware defects, AI and software failures, software update impacts, third-party integration failures, supervision and operator failures, bodily injury, property and equipment damage, business interruption, cyber incidents, vendor failure, the contract names which party bears the loss. No standardized contractual model exists across the industry right now, which makes the absence of a standard your opportunity to set the terms.

An insurable deployment confirms coverage in writing instead of assuming it. Existing product liability, commercial general liability, and workers' compensation policies often do not cover AI-caused physical losses, and the exclusions are spreading. Disclose the model dependencies, document the testing program, and get affirmative confirmation from the insurer. If the carrier will not confirm, you are self-insuring. Price the deployment accordingly.

An insurable deployment knows its fleet. Unit identities, hardware manifests, pinned software versions, model backbones, producible on demand. And it knows its model concentration: how many units share a backbone, and what the correlated scenario looks like. A software bill of materials is not an IT nicety here. It is an underwriting input.

An insurable deployment gates its updates. Over-the-air model updates are the highest-correlation events in the system: a single push can change the behavior of the entire fleet simultaneously. Staged rollouts, canary fleets, explicit authorization at the fleet level, rollback rights in the vendor contract. No silent fleet-wide pushes, ever.

An uninsurable deployment is the mirror image: entangled planning and actuation, no per-action records, updates pushed silently, unknown model concentration, coverage assumed rather than confirmed, liability allocation left to the post-incident argument. That is the three-vendor finger-pointing exercise, and every enterprise buyer has already lived through the IT version of it. They are not volunteering for the physical version.

Price your risk before the market does

One more move, and it is the one most founders skip: start pricing your risk per action now, even if no insurer is involved yet. Compute the risk price for every action, accumulate the ledger, report it. Call it shadow pricing. It gives you three things at once. Operational visibility into which tasks, robots, and conditions are expensive, denominated in money, in real time. A dataset nobody else has. And the actuarial evidence base you will need when the carrier conversation happens. You will arrive with a year of priced action data. Nobody else will have that.

Annual policies price the average action, and the average lies. A fleet's risk concentrates in specific actions, specific shifts, specific software versions. Per-action visibility forces the true cost of risky behavior into the open, which is the only place it can change behavior.

Boring wins the deal

A final commercial note, because engineering without a sale is a hobby. Enterprise risk committees do not want to learn your novel governance framework. They want to see controls mapped to frameworks their auditors already recognize: the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, SOC 2 attestations, and their equivalents. Novel governance is a sales objection. Boring, recognized compliance is a sales accelerator.

Enterprise deals die in risk review more often than they die in technical evaluation. A compliance package that answers the risk committee's questions in advance takes weeks off the longest phase of the deal. And once one vendor in a category arrives with a real package, it becomes table stakes for the category. The vendors that treated governance as phase two discover they are bidding against vendors that treated it as phase zero. Phase zero wins.

Compliance is not safety, and you should say that out loud. A SOC 2 report does not make a robot safe. It proves you run the controls you claim to run. Your authorization records prove what the fleet actually did. The first gets you through procurement. The second protects you both after deployment. Buyers can tell the difference, and the honesty is itself an accelerator.

What to do Monday morning

Start the append-only record. Whatever your fleet size, whatever your architecture, start logging every action proposal, every authorization decision, and every outcome, in a form nobody can rewrite. It costs almost nothing. It is the foundation every other move in this essay stands on. The operators who have the record when the incident, the auditor, or the regulator arrives will be glad they do. The ones who do not will be writing it from memory, and memory is the least reliable log format ever invented.
