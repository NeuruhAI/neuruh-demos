Your Prompt Has Physics

Your prompt has physics.

Change the first line of a prompt that is running in production and you just invalidated the key-value cache behind it. The model throws out its stored state and recomputes from scratch. Throughput dies. Latency spikes. Cost multiplies. It is the AI equivalent of rebuilding the engine because you repainted the hood.

Almost nobody running agents in production knows this. They should. The physics of the system you are standing on decides what it costs you to change your mind.

I read a paper recently, one of mine, that frames the whole thing as physics instead of software. The argument in one line: the "model" is dead. Context is the new king. Parameter counts were the 2023 conversation. By 2026 they are a relic. What matters is architecture: how information is organized, how it flows, and what it costs to touch it.

The three-layer brain

The paper proposes a liquid context architecture with three layers. An immutable identity layer that never moves. An append-only history that only grows. A dynamic tail that gets rewritten constantly.

Here is why that matters to your bill: the identity and history layers sit warm in the cache. The tail is the only thing you are paying to recompute. Prefix stability is not a style choice. It is a cost structure. Every prompt edit that touches the front of the context is a financial decision, whether the person editing it knows it or not.

I have a standing rule now. The stable stuff goes first and stays first. The volatile stuff goes last. Touch the front of a production prompt the way you would touch the foundation of a building: deliberately, rarely, and with a reason you can defend.

Novelty is the solvent

The same paper has a section on the biology of novelty, and it connects to the most durable thing I know about business. The brain releases BDNF, a fertilizer for neurons, when it encounters genuine novelty. Without it, the brain carves energy ruts: the same paths, the same responses, the same comfortable inefficiency, worn deeper every day.

I spent 25 years in estate cleanouts, salvage, and thrift arbitrage. That work is nothing but ruts other people carved. The value hides exactly where the original owner stopped looking: the box nobody opened, the room nobody priced, the category everyone walks past. Novelty is the only solvent I have ever found for that blindness. You have to walk into rooms that are not on your route.

The physics point and the biology point are the same point. A system that never gets new context, or only gets it at the wrong layer, degrades. A model running on a stale cache gets more expensive the longer you leave it alone. A founder running on a stale routine makes the same mispriced decisions with increasing confidence. In both cases the cure is the same: fresh context, placed where it costs the least to use.

The part everyone skips

The paper lists six counter-intuitive truths and I will not walk through all of them. The one that decides your margins is T4: context beats parameters. The team that obsesses over which model they are using and ignores how their context is structured is polishing the chip while ignoring the board. The chip is the cheapest part of the machine. The board is where the money goes.

Run your agents like they have physics, because they do. Put the stable stuff first. Keep history append-only. Touch the front of a production prompt like it costs money, because it does. And when the work starts feeling cheap and familiar, go find a room you have never walked into. The BDNF is the point.
