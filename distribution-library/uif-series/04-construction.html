Construction — Never Let a Language Model Do Date Math on a Lien Deadline

For: specialty subcontractors and suppliers — electrical, mechanical, concrete, drywall Alt headline: The Deadline You Didn't Know You Missed Keyword: mechanics lien deadline tracking

Lien rights run on a clock. Preliminary notices, notices of intent, and lien filings each have deadlines, and they start from different events: first day on site, first delivery, last day of work, project completion. Miss one and the right can be gone, regardless of how good the work was. So a sub sets up an AI assistant to read contracts and invoices and track the dates. It reads "first furnished" off the wrong delivery ticket. It counts 90 days from the invoice date instead of the last day of work. It never sees the change order that extended the job. The calendar looks managed. It isn't.

The field that broke: Dates — specifically, dates the model calculated instead of dates it read.

The missing pins: The trigger and the fuse. Nothing defined which event starts which clock, and nothing stopped a computed date from landing on the calendar.

The fix: The model reads dates; it never adds days. Code computes each deadline from a state rules table. That rules table is reviewed by your construction attorney, not written by an AI and not by us. Each tracked date cites its document: delivery ticket, daily log, change order. New documents trigger a recheck. A change order on day 60 updates every downstream clock. Reminders fire at 14 and 7 days out, and anything ambiguous goes to a person, flagged red. This isn't legal advice, and the system doesn't replace your attorney. It replaces the spreadsheet nobody updated after the change order.

Where else this shows up: Any clock that starts from an event has this shape: claim reporting windows, denial appeal deadlines (Healthcare Admin), bid due dates, certification renewals. A model is good at finding the event in a document. It's the wrong part for counting days from it.
