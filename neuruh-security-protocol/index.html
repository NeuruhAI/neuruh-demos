1.1 The Chase correction

What was false. A viral post circulating on X and Facebook on November 16, 2026 claimed JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon had confirmed a coordinated, state-sponsored cyberattack traced to North Korean and Russian threat actors. Dimon said no such thing. No outlet reported such a statement, and the FBI said the opposite. A separate claim that the November 16 outages at Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, and other banks were a coordinated cyberattack was also false. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton said on November 17, 2026 that the disruptions were not a cyberattack and not a coordinated effort by a nation state. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said on November 16 and 17, 2026 that he was 98 percent certain the outages were not a cyberattack, and later attributed the series to aging computer systems with new technology layered on top.

What was true. On Monday, November 16, 2026, JPMorgan Chase had a software bug in its on-premise hardware systems, traced by an FBI official to a software release the bank rolled out earlier that day. Chase said there was no evidence it was cyber-related, that customers still had access to their accounts and funds, and that transactions were posting normally again by about 6:30 p.m. ET on November 16. Other banks appeared affected only where Chase was a party to a transaction. A real, separate cyberattack did occur the same week: a supply-chain script injection through Optimizely that reached 178 organizations. It was not connected to the Chase outage. A ransomware group called n0n also listed JPMorgan Chase on its leak site on November 16, 2026. That is a criminal group's claim, not a confirmed breach, and Chase has not confirmed any connection to the outage.

Note on Secretary Mullin. On Fox News on November 16 he also floated an unsupported theory, attributed to one unnamed tech contact, that artificial intelligence or "super intelligence" was behind the outages. No company involved attributed its outage to AI, and on November 17 he reframed the cause as old systems overwhelmed by new technology. The "not a cyberattack" finding is confirmed. The AI-caused-outages theory is not.

2. Industry Study: What the AI Era Requires

Research only. No scanning, probing, contacting, posting, or sending was done for this study. Every framework and incident claim below carries a named source with a date. Figures are quoted from those sources as reported; where two sources disagree, the disagreement is noted rather than smoothed over. Scope note: this study is written for a small operator, not an enterprise. "Good" in each family is stated at that scale. The AI era does not invent new control families. It shortens the time between a weakness and its exploitation, and it makes impersonation cheap. Controls that were good practice in 2023 are now the minimum.

2.1 NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0

Source: NIST, "The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0", CSWP 29, published 26 February 2024. CSF 2.0 is a voluntary framework of cybersecurity outcomes organized into six Functions, 22 Categories, and 106 Subcategories. It describes what a program should achieve, not specific technical controls, and it is not a certification. The headline change from version 1.1 is the addition of Govern as a sixth Function, placed at the center, with supply chain risk management moved under it.

Table 3. NIST CSF 2.0 functions in the AI era
FunctionWhat it demands in the AI era
Govern (GV)Strategy, roles, policy, and oversight are set, communicated, and monitored by whoever is accountable. In the AI era this means a named decision-maker for every AI tool and agent, a written rule on what AI systems may and may not do unsupervised, and supply chain oversight (GV.SC) that covers AI coding tools and third-party agents, not just vendors.
Identify (ID)Current risks are understood: a complete asset inventory, a risk assessment, and an improvement loop. In the AI era the inventory must include AI agents, API keys, tokens, browser profiles, and every device that can reach a signing or payment function, because an unlisted asset is an unmonitored entry point.
Protect (PR)Safeguards are used to manage risk: identity and access control, awareness and training, data security, platform security, and infrastructure resilience. In the AI era Protect means phishing-resistant authentication, least privilege on every token, and no plaintext credential anywhere a sync or a repo can reach.
Detect (DE)Possible attacks and compromises are found and analyzed through continuous monitoring and adverse event analysis. In the AI era detection must be fast enough to catch machine-speed attacks: an AI-driven intrusion does not wait for a weekly log review.
Respond (RS)Action is taken on a detected incident: management, analysis, reporting and communication, and mitigation. In the AI era Respond means a tested kill switch that can revoke tokens, freeze a signing service, and cut off a compromised tool within minutes, not a plan that is written down and never rehearsed.
Recover (RC)Assets and operations affected by an incident are restored, and recovery is communicated. In the AI era Recover means tested offline backups and a rebuild path for every compromised device and credential, because an attacker who survives a patch can survive the recovery too.

Failure mode it prevents: a program with no center. Without Govern, each tool is secured on its own and nobody owns the whole picture, so a compromised developer laptop becomes a compromised wallet, as the Binance case in family 4 shows. Good for a small operator: one written page naming who decides, what the rules are, and what happens when something goes wrong, reviewed on a fixed schedule.

2.2 Zero Trust Architecture

Source: NIST Special Publication 800-207, "Zero Trust Architecture", published August 2020. Zero trust is a security model built on "never trust, always verify": no user, device, or network segment is trusted by default, and every access request is authenticated, authorized, and encrypted before it is granted. NIST frames it around a Policy Decision Point that evaluates identity, device posture, and context, and a Policy Enforcement Point that grants or denies access. Its tenets include per-session access, dynamic policy, continuous monitoring, and strict enforcement before access is granted.

Table 4. Zero trust elements
ElementWhat it isFailure mode it prevents
Identity verificationEvery access request is tied to a verified identity, with strong authentication (MFA, preferably phishing-resistant such as FIDO2 passkeys) as the primary control plane.A stolen password alone opening the whole estate. Verizon's 2026 DBIR, as summarized in 2026 reporting, puts stolen credentials at 13 percent of initial access, still a large share, and credential theft is the entry point in the Binance and Bybit cases.
Least privilegeEach identity, human or machine, gets only the access its role needs, for only as long as it needs it.A single over-scoped token becoming a master key. In the Binance incident, a container deployment permission token sitting on an engineer's computer was the step that let attackers deploy a container and reach the wallet signing service (Binance initial report, 29 October 2026).
Continuous verificationTrust is re-evaluated through the whole session, not once at login. A new location, a failed device posture check, or anomalous behavior can step up or revoke access mid-session.A session hijack or a compromised device staying trusted for the rest of the day. AI-accelerated attacks move laterally in minutes; a login-time check is already stale by the time the damage is done.
Micro-segmentationThe network and the workload estate are divided into small, individually governed zones, so traffic between them is restricted by default.Lateral movement. A compromised web server or a compromised developer laptop should not be able to reach a signing service or a database it has no legitimate business talking to.

Why perimeter thinking fails against AI-accelerated attacks: the perimeter assumes the danger is outside and the inside is safe. AI removes the two things that assumption relied on. First, phishing and impersonation now scale: AI-written messages, cloned voices, and deepfake video let an attacker walk through the front door as a trusted insider, so "inside the network" no longer means "trusted". Second, once inside, automation moves at machine speed: the Binance attackers went from a compromised laptop to control of a signing service across three engineers' machines, and the funds left in about 20 minutes (Binance initial report; Dark Reading analysis, November 2026). A firewall cannot see that; only per-request verification and segmentation can slow it. Good for a small operator: MFA on every account, one admin account that is never used for daily work, separate browser profiles for admin and general use, and no service reachable from a general-purpose laptop that does not need to be.

2.3 Secrets and Credential Hygiene

Source: GitGuardian, "State of Secrets Sprawl 2026" report, as reported in 2026. GitGuardian recorded 29 million new secrets exposed in public GitHub repositories in 2025, up 34 percent year over year, and found that 64 percent of secrets confirmed valid in 2022 were still valid and unrevoked as of January 2026. AI-assisted commits leak secrets at roughly twice the baseline rate. Internal repositories are six times more likely to contain a hardcoded credential than public ones: 32.2 percent of internal repositories contain at least one secret, against 5.6 percent of public repositories. Long-lived secrets that are never rotated account for 60 percent of all policy violations tracked.

Supporting source: Truffle Security, 2026 study of The Stack v3 dataset (224,553,295 GitHub repositories, dataset closed 7 August 2025). Researchers tested detected credentials on 27 and 28 July 2026 and found 543,699 unique credentials that still authenticated successfully. The median exposed credential had been in public code for 784 days, and 10 percent were over 6.3 years old. The oldest working credential dated to June 2009, a set of database credentials in an Erlang web server configuration file, still valid 16.1 years later.

Table 5. Secrets and credential hygiene practices
PracticeWhat it isFailure mode it prevents
RotationEvery credential has an expiry and a rotation schedule, and rotation is tested, not assumed.The 16-year-old credential. A secret that never expires never has to be defended against a changing threat model, and the GitGuardian and Truffle findings show that unrotated secrets stay valid for years after they leak.
VaultingSecrets live in a dedicated vault or secret manager, never in code, config files, notes, or chat.The copy that escapes. A credential in a synced folder, a shared drive, or a repo is a credential that has already been distributed to every device and backup that syncs it.
No plaintext credentials in synced folders or reposNo key, token, or password in any file that syncs, backs up, or commits.The Binance pattern at small scale: a token on a laptop is a token an attacker can harvest the moment the laptop is compromised. The Shai-Hulud npm worm, analyzed by GitGuardian in 2026, harvested credentials from the local environment and used them to publish malicious package versions.
Token scopingEvery token is scoped to the smallest permission set and the smallest resource set that lets the job run.The over-scoped deploy token. The Binance attackers found a container deployment permission token on an engineer's computer; scope is what turns a stolen token into a contained incident instead of a total loss.
RevocationA written, tested revocation path exists for every credential class, and revocation is fast.The authorization that outlives the breach. In the Binance incident, EIP-7702 on-chain authorizations kept working for about four hours after Binance suspended withdrawals, because suspending the service did not invalidate existing authorizations (Dark Reading, November 2026). Revocation must be tested against the actual mechanism, not assumed.

Good for a small operator: every secret in a vault, every token scoped and short-lived where the platform allows it, a dated rotation list, and a one-page revocation checklist that has actually been run once.

2.4 Supply-Chain Security

Source: Binance, "29 October 2026 Binance Security Incident: Initial Findings and Response Update", published on binance.com. The FBI has attributed the incident to the Democratic People's Republic of Korea. Binance describes it as a sophisticated, multi-stage campaign, not a single breach or a simple phishing incident, combining social engineering by fake recruitment interviews, malicious software downloads, compromised internal AI tooling, endpoint compromise, and an advanced transaction-authorization method. About 6.34 billion US dollars in digital assets were stolen, the largest cryptocurrency theft on record as reported in November 2026, and about 2.85 billion dollars had been frozen or intercepted as of 31 October 2026.

Case study, the attack chain as Binance reports it:

  1. Supply chain attack. An engineer on the Wallet DevOps team downloaded a tampered Homebrew installation while trying to install a popular AI coding tool after searching for it on a search engine. Binance detected the anomaly, replaced the device, and rotated related credentials, but the engineer's role and his wallet-infrastructure colleagues were exposed to the attacker.
  2. Fake recruitment interview. A second engineer ran an assignment from a fake job interview on a work computer, and the malware got past endpoint protection. This matches the North Korean TraderTraitor pattern also seen in the Bybit theft of 21 February 2025, where attackers compromised a Safe{Wallet} developer's Mac nearly three weeks before the theft (Dark Reading, November 2026).
  3. Internal AI tool as springboard. On 20 October 2026, the attacker used the compromised computer of one engineer and an internal AI tool to gain control over two more engineers, and found a container deployment permission token on one engineer's computer.
  4. Signing service. On 29 October 2026, the attacker reached the internal wallet signing service and used it to exploit Ethereum's EIP-7702 authorization mechanism, turning a hot wallet into an attacker-controlled sweeper. Most of the value left in about 20 minutes.

Supporting source: the tj-actions/changed-files supply chain attack, March 2025 (CVE-2025-30066). An attacker modified tags v1 through v45.0.7 to reference a malicious commit, and the action, used in over 23,000 repositories, dumped CI secrets into workflow logs. CISA added CVE-2025-30066 to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 26 March 2025. The root cause traced to reviewdog/action-setup@v1, compromised on 11 March 2025 (CVE-2025-30154).

Table 6. Supply-chain elements
ElementWhat it isFailure mode it prevents
Third-party toolingEvery tool, package, installer, and AI coding assistant is treated as code that runs with your privileges. Installers come from the vendor's official source, never from a search result.The tampered Homebrew installer. The single most expensive download in the Binance chain was a package manager fetched after a web search.

2.5 Comms and Messaging Security

Source: FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3), 2025 Internet Crime Report, released April 2026. The report tracked AI-related fraud as its own category for the first time: 22,364 complaints mentioning AI, with about 893 million dollars in reported losses. AI-enabled business email compromise topped 30 million dollars in 2025. Total reported internet crime losses were nearly 21 billion dollars across 1,008,597 complaints.

Supporting sources: CrowdStrike 2025 Global Threat Report, which found voice phishing (vishing) rose 442 percent from the first half to the second half of 2024, driven by AI-driven phishing and impersonation. FBI public service announcement, May 2025, warning that since April 2025 criminals have sent texts and AI-generated voice messages impersonating senior US officials, and advising victims to call back on an independently verified number and to agree on a secret word or phrase with people they trust.

2.7 Endpoint Hardening

2.8 Incident Response and Audit Logging

Source: NIST SP 800-61 Revision 3, "Incident Response Recommendations and Considerations for Cybersecurity Risk Management: A CSF 2.0 Community Profile", published April 2025. Revision 3 replaces the four-phase lifecycle of Revision 2 (2012), preparation, detection and analysis, containment, eradication and recovery, and post-incident activity, with incident response mapped onto the six functions of CSF 2.0. It treats incident response as part of the wider risk management cycle, not a standalone playbook.

Supporting source: OWASP Logging Cheat Sheet, the standard practitioner reference, which states that audit logs must never contain passwords, session identifiers, access tokens, encryption keys, or connection strings, and that an audit trail must be complete for its scope, immutable, attributable to an authenticated identity, and retained for a defined period. PCI DSS, cited in the same reference, requires audit log history retained for at least twelve months, with the most recent three months immediately available for analysis.

Table 10. Incident response and audit logging elements
ElementWhat it isFailure mode it prevents
Hash-chained receiptsEach log or receipt record includes a hash of the previous record, so any alteration, deletion, or reordering breaks the chain and is detectable. Records are stored append-only, with restricted write access, and a signed digest is periodically anchored elsewhere.The rewritten history. An attacker who can edit the log can erase the evidence of how they got in, and an operator who cannot prove the record is intact cannot prove what happened. Hash chaining makes tampering detectable even if the log store itself is compromised.
Immutable logsLogs are written once and cannot be modified or deleted within their retention window, and log administration is separate from the systems being logged.The blind investigation. If the same administrator who runs a compromised service can also delete its logs, the investigation starts with nothing. Separation of log storage from the logged system is the control.
Detection speedMonitoring is continuous and alerts are acted on, not batched into a weekly review. Time from first malicious action to detection is measured.The 20-minute theft. In the Binance incident, most of the value left in about 20 minutes, and the EIP-7702 authorizations kept working for about four hours after countermeasures began (Dark Reading, November 2026). Detection that arrives after the money is gone is not detection; it is a post-mortem.
Time-to-containA tested kill switch exists: revoke tokens, freeze the signing or payment function, isolate the compromised device, and cut off the compromised tool. Containment time is measured in rehearsal, not guessed in the incident.The authorization that outlives the response. Binance's own report notes that suspending withdrawals and shutting down hot wallet services did not automatically invalidate existing on-chain authorizations; additional transactions were needed to revoke them. Dark Reading's analysis (November 2026) reports that experts faulted the absence of a pretested kill switch to disable malicious authorizations quickly. A kill switch that has never been tested is a hope, not a control.

Good for a small operator: an append-only, hash-chained log of every consequential action (who, what, when, which credential), stored where the operator of the logged system cannot delete it, a one-page incident runbook with a tested kill switch, and a rehearsal that measures minutes from alert to containment.

5.1 Name

The Neuruh Verify-Before-You-Act Protocol. The name states the rule in plain words. Nothing that moves money, access, credentials, deployments, or a public claim happens on the strength of a single unverified message or a single agent output. It is verified first, then it acts, then the act leaves a receipt. Short name for everyday use: Verify Before You Act. Abbreviation for file and receipt labels: VBYA.

5.2 Architecture: five spines built from existing primitives

The protocol does not invent a new security stack. It wires five things Neuruh already has into one order of operations: verify identity, check authority, get a verdict, get founder approval where required, and only then let anything move to deployment. Each spine below states what it is as verified in the sources, what it does inside this protocol, and its current state with the evidence. Where a spine is blocked or only a simulation, the protocol says so and names the human fallback that covers the gap until the spine is real.

The order of operations for any consequential action is fixed:

  1. Identity: N-Cell authority header names who or what is acting and under what authority.
  2. Authority: G-1 founder approval decides whether the action is authorized at all.
  3. Verdict: Sentinel (or, while Sentinel is blocked, a named human verdict) allows, escalates, or denies.
  4. Custody and promotion: the custody and promotion gates decide whether the result may move to deployment or publication.
  5. Receipt: CRUCIBLE hash chaining records the whole sequence so any party can recompute it later.

No step may be skipped because an earlier step looked fine. A valid identity header does not imply authorization. Authorization does not imply a passing verdict. A passing verdict does not imply promotion.

5.3 The comms and messaging layer

This section gets the most detail because it is the core complaint: Jeramie receives messages that claim to be from someone, or that claim Jeramie said something, on channels where anyone can write anything, and there is today no agreed way to tell a real message from a fake one before acting on it. The layer below is designed so that no consequential action ever rests on a single message on a single channel, and so that every message that does cause an action can be traced back to a verification step and a receipt.

Definitions used in this section:

  • A consequential action is anything that moves money, changes access or credentials, changes a deployment or a publication, commits Jeramie to a deal or a call, or sends a message in his name.
  • A verified channel is a channel where the sender's identity has been established by a prior out-of-band check and where the channel itself cannot be trivially spoofed by a third party writing a display name. No channel is verified by default. Facebook Messenger, X direct messages, email from an unfamiliar address, and any group thread are unverified until proven otherwise for that sender.
  • A counterparty is any named person or organization Jeramie does business with, including a buyer, a seller, a partner, a vendor, a platform, or a service provider.
  • The identity sheet is the single written list of Jeramie's real channels and each counterparty's verified contact points. It is a Phase 0 deliverable. Until it exists, every sender is unverified.

5.3.1 Message authentication: how a recipient knows a message is really from Jeramie or from a named counterparty

Authentication has three layers, and a consequential action needs all three. A routine message needs only the first.

Layer 1: channel check. The message must arrive on a channel listed for that sender on the identity sheet. If Jeramie's real contact sheet says a counterparty is reached by phone at a named number and by email at a named address, then a message from that counterparty on Messenger is not authenticated by channel, even if the display name matches. Display names, profile photos, and familiar writing style are not authentication. They are the easiest things to copy.

Layer 2: shared secret check. Each counterparty relationship carries a rotating code word, agreed out of band and recorded only on the identity sheet and in Jeramie's own notes, never in the message thread itself. For any consequential request, the sender must include the current code word, or answer a challenge with it. The code word rotates on a fixed schedule (monthly for routine counterparties, per transaction for money counterparties) and immediately after any suspected compromise. Muse never invents a code word, never guesses one, and never accepts "I forgot the code word, just trust me" as authentication. A failed code word is a failed authentication, not a prompt to try a different channel.

Layer 3: content check. The message must be consistent with what is already known: the deal, the amount, the parcel, the deadline, the prior thread. A message that introduces a new payment address, a new amount, a new deadline, or a new instruction that contradicts the prior record fails the content check even if layers 1 and 2 pass, and it is escalated for out-of-band confirmation under Section 5.3.2. Content that is only consistent because it quotes back public information does not pass on its own; public facts are available to an impersonator too.

How Jeramie authenticates himself to others. The same three layers run in reverse. When Muse sends or drafts a message in Jeramie's name, it uses only a channel listed on the identity sheet for Jeramie, it includes the code word only when the counterparty relationship requires it and Jeramie has supplied it for that message, and it never introduces a new payment address, amount, or commitment that is not already in the verified record. If a counterparty asks Muse to prove a message is really from Jeramie, Muse does not argue in the thread. It points the counterparty to the out-of-band check in Section 5.3.2.

What Muse does with an incoming message that asks Muse to act. Muse reads the message, checks it against the identity sheet and the three layers, and records the result. If any layer fails, Muse does not act on the message. It reports the failure to Jeramie with the exact text of the message, the channel it arrived on, and which layer failed. Muse never treats a message that says "Jeramie told me to tell you" as an instruction from Jeramie. A relayed instruction is an unauthenticated message from the relayer, not an instruction from Jeramie.

5.3.2 Out-of-band confirmation for money or access changes

Out-of-band confirmation is mandatory, without exception, for these five cases:

  1. Any payment, refund, payout, or change to where money goes, including a new wallet address, bank account, payment link, or Stripe destination.
  2. Any change to credentials, passwords, API keys, tokens, or account recovery settings, including a password reset link or a request to share a code.
  3. Any change to who has access to an account, a repo, a deployment, a team, or a workspace, including adding or removing a collaborator.
  4. Any commitment to a price, a deadline, or a deal term that differs from the last verified record.
  5. Any instruction to delete, revoke, or destroy something that cannot be undone.

The confirmation procedure is fixed:

  • Step 1: Muse or Jeramie states the exact change in writing, in one message, with the old value and the new value side by side. No paraphrase. The old value is taken from the last verified record, not from the message that proposed the change.
  • Step 2: The confirmation happens on a second channel that is different from the channel the request arrived on, and that is listed on the identity sheet for that party. A request that arrived by Messenger is confirmed by a phone call to the number on the identity sheet, or by email to the address on the identity sheet. A request that arrived by email is confirmed by phone. Two messages on the same channel are not out-of-band confirmation.
  • Step 3: The confirming party repeats back the exact change, including the exact destination or value, in their own words on the second channel. "Yes, do it" is not confirmation. "Yes, send it to the new address ending in the digits I read back" is confirmation.
  • Step 4: The confirmation is written to the receipt chain as a receipt entry naming both channels, the time of each message, and who confirmed what. The action proceeds only after that receipt entry exists.
  • Step 5: If the second channel cannot be reached, or the party cannot repeat the change back, the action does not happen. It waits. No deadline in the original message overrides this. A message that says "confirm in the next ten minutes or the deal dies" is treated as a pressure tactic and is escalated, not obeyed.

One-time codes have their own rule. A sign-in code, an authenticator code, or a recovery code is never read out over a channel that asked for it, never pasted into a thread, and never forwarded to a third party. A code is used only by the person signing in, on the device and session that requested it, for the site named in the challenge. If a message asks Muse or Jeramie to share a code, that message is treated as unauthenticated and is reported, not obeyed.

5.3.3 What to do when a message arrives on a channel that cannot be verified

A channel that cannot be verified is not a channel to argue on. It is a channel to contain. The procedure:

  1. Do not act on the message. Do not send money, change access, share a code, approve a deal, or reply in Jeramie's name on the strength of it.
  2. Do not reply in the same thread with sensitive information. A reply that says "that is not my real address, my real address is X" hands the attacker the real address. A reply that says "I never said that" confirms that someone is listening.
  3. Preserve the message. Screenshot or copy the full text, the sender display name, the channel, the time, and any links or attachments, and file it in the security receipt log with a receipt entry. Preservation is evidence, and it costs nothing.
  4. Verify through the identity sheet. Contact the claimed sender on a verified channel from the sheet, using a contact point that was recorded before this message arrived, and ask one question: "Did you send this?" Do not forward the suspicious message in full; describe it in general terms so you do not spread a malicious link or a false claim.
  5. If the claimed sender says no, or cannot be reached, treat the message as impersonation. Record that verdict in the receipt log. If the message asked for money or access, tell Jeramie the same day with the preserved copy attached. If the message is a duplicate of a real message the sender did send, record that it is a duplicate and act only on the verified original.
  6. If the claimed sender says yes, ask them to resend the request through a verified channel with the current code word, and act only on that resend. The original unverified message is never acted on directly, even after the sender confirms it, because acting on the original rewards the channel instead of the verification.
  7. If the same unverified channel produces a second suspicious message, the channel is marked UNTRUSTED on the identity sheet, and all future messages from it are routed to the same procedure without exception. The mark is removed only by Jeramie, in writing, after a fresh out-of-band check.

Group threads get one extra rule. A message in a group thread is never treated as a private instruction from any member of the group, because any member can quote or impersonate another member's words in the thread. A consequential request that appears in a group thread must be restated by the requester in a one-to-one verified channel before it is acted on.

5.3.4 How receipts close the loop

Every message that leads to an action, and every message that was refused, gets a receipt entry in the security receipt log. The entry records:

  • The receipt ID and the hash of the previous receipt in the chain.
  • The date and time in America/Detroit, and the channel the message arrived on.
  • The claimed sender and the result of each of the three authentication layers.
  • The exact request, in the sender's words, shortened only by removing secrets and credentials.
  • The out-of-band confirmation, if any: second channel, time, and what was repeated back.
  • The verdict: ALLOW, ESCALATE, or DENY, and who issued it (Jeramie, Muse on explicit instruction, or Sentinel once unblocked).
  • The action taken or not taken, and the G-1 authorization it rested on.
  • The custody and promotion release, if the action produced something public.

The loop is closed only when the counterparty confirms receipt of the result on a verified channel, or when a refusal is acknowledged by Jeramie. An action with no confirmation back is recorded as UNCONFIRMED, not as complete. The chain is verified on a fixed schedule (weekly in Phase 2, daily once Phase 3 starts) by re-running the verifier over the full log. A failed verification halts new consequential actions until the break is explained in a receipt entry.

The receipt log itself lives in two places: the working copy under ~/workspace/neuruh-security-protocol/ and a read-only copy whose hash is recorded separately, so that a tampered working copy can be detected by comparison. The log contains no secret values, only names of secrets and the fact of their rotation. Anyone who can read the log can verify the chain. No one needs a credential to verify it.

5.3.5 The standing rules, in one place

  1. One message on one channel never moves money, access, or a deployment.
  2. A display name is not an identity. A familiar writing style is not an identity.
  3. A relayed instruction ("Jeramie said") is not an instruction from Jeramie.
  4. Urgency is a reason to verify more carefully, never a reason to verify less.
  5. A failed authentication is reported, not retried through a different channel.
  6. A refusal is receipted the same way an approval is. Silence is not a verdict.
  7. When in doubt, the action waits and Jeramie decides. Waiting is the safe default, and it is never treated as a failure.

B.9 The privacy and information sovereignty plane (PROPOSED)

Governance must cover what agents can know, retain, expose and transfer, not only whether they can act. Information Sovereignty and Privacy Governance (ISPG) is proposed as an independent plane. Capabilities, 18: data classification; personal information detection; sensitive data segregation; purpose limitation; data minimization; consent and legal-basis tracking where applicable; retention schedules; deletion verification; data residency; cross-tenant isolation; secret detection; output redaction; external disclosure authorization; training-data restrictions; evidence access controls; legal hold; data lineage; third-party data-sharing restrictions.

Two warnings the proposal states plainly. First, the evidence ledger must not become the largest repository of sensitive information in the company. Receipts should reference protected evidence objects through controlled identifiers and integrity digests, rather than embedding the sensitive data itself. Second, immutability must be reconciled with privacy, retention and deletion obligations. An immutable digest can remain while the underlying sensitive evidence is lawfully deleted, provided the deletion itself is recorded and independently verifiable.

B.10 Governance of governance (PROPOSED)

B.11 The Five Interrogations (PROPOSED)

Every protected action gets a structured interrogation, not merely a permission check.

  1. WHAT IS TRUE? Identity, evidence, provenance, context, freshness, uncertainty and contradictions.
  2. WHAT IS PERMITTED? Authority, delegation, mission, policy, boundaries and approvals.
  3. WHAT COULD HAPPEN? Failure modes, dependencies, attack surfaces, downstream effects and unintended consequences.
  4. WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED? Execution, observed state, independent verification, evidence and custody.
  5. WHAT MUST CHANGE? Recovery, corrective action, learning, policy revision, risk reduction and regression testing.

The same five questions can govern a property acquisition; a financial transaction; a software deployment; a research report; a public communication; a physical-world operation; an autonomous agent spawning another agent; a new model entering the Neuruh ecosystem. Different risk profiles, same fundamental governance structure.

B.12 Seven overlooked problems (PROPOSED)

Less obvious, but potentially more important than another 50 features, in the proposal's own words.

  1. The unknown-unknown problem. A system can only detect failures it has some mechanism for recognizing. What about a failure nobody anticipated? Needed: anomaly detection independent of predefined rules; novelty detection; behavioral baseline comparison; unexpected dependency discovery; unclassified-event quarantine; human investigation pathways; automatic creation of new test candidates from novel failures. The objective is not to pretend unknown unknowns can be eliminated. It is to make the system recognize when its existing knowledge is insufficient.
  2. The negative-space problem. Records show what happened. Do they record what should have happened but did not? Examples: an expected approval never arrived; a receipt was never generated; an agent stopped reporting; a scheduled verification did not run; a deployment never reached its final state; an expected data source disappeared; an agent skipped a mandatory dependency. The proposal calls for a Missing Event Intelligence System. Absence of an expected event should be observable and actionable.
  3. The common-mode failure problem. What happens when three independent safeguards all depend on the same compromised database? They are not really independent. Dependency independence analysis is needed. If identity, authorization and audit all rely on one compromised service, three passing checks may represent one failure, not three independent confirmations.
  4. The recovery paradox. What happens when the security system itself needs to be recovered? Who authorizes recovery if the authority registry is unavailable? Where are the emergency credentials stored? How do we prove the restored system has not inherited compromised state? This requires independently protected recovery credentials, tested restoration procedures, offline evidence, and an independent verification path that does not depend on the system being recovered.
  5. The trust contamination problem. A trusted agent can consume untrusted data. An approved artifact can incorporate an unverified dependency. A legitimate agent can inherit corrupted context. Trust must not automatically transfer through relationships. Explicit trust inheritance rules and contamination propagation are needed.
  6. The decision debt problem. Every unresolved assumption, deferred verification, temporary exception and accepted risk accumulates as a form of debt. The proposal calls for a Governance Debt Registry tracking 9 items: unresolved security findings; expired exceptions; temporary permissions; deferred verification; unreviewed policy changes; unvalidated assumptions; repeated incidents; accumulated technical debt; unproven controls. A system should not look healthy merely because nobody has closed the tickets.
  7. The adversarial economics problem. Attackers and misaligned agents adapt. They may discover that the most effective way around a control is not to defeat it, but to exploit its economics. Examples, 5: flood the founder with approval requests; exhaust an agent's execution budget; create enough harmless alerts to hide one serious alert; exploit a gap between two services; make an approval process so cumbersome that humans bypass it. Economic and behavioral adversarial testing is needed, not only technical penetration tests.

B.13 The Governance Research Engine (PROPOSED)

Instead of only responding to failures, VBYA could continuously generate controlled hypotheses about its own weaknesses. The 12-step experiment, as stated in the proposal: identify a control; identify the assumption behind that control; generate a possible failure scenario; construct a safe test environment; attempt to violate the assumption; record whether the control detects it; measure the detection delay; determine whether the shadow system catches it; create a remediation proposal; require approval before changing production governance; re-run the regression suite; preserve the complete experiment. The Autonomous Assurance Research Loop, 8 steps: observe the organism; identify assumptions and weaknesses; generate adversarial hypotheses; test in isolated environments; measure control effectiveness; create findings and remediation; human and independent governance approval; regression verification and learning. All experimentation must remain inside authorized, isolated test boundaries. VBYA does not just govern the organism. It continuously investigates whether its governance is sufficient.

B.16 The Universal Capability Assurance Contract (PROPOSED)

One universal capability contract. Every Neuruh capability must declare the following 20 points. No capability gets promoted to production merely because its code exists. It must demonstrate its operational behavior, failure behavior, authority boundaries and evidence quality.

  1. What is its purpose?
  2. What does it consume?
  3. What does it produce?
  4. What authority does it require?
  5. What assumptions does it make?
  6. What dependencies does it have?
  7. What can make it fail?
  8. What can make it lie?
  9. How can it be manipulated?
  10. How can its output be independently verified?
  11. What happens when it becomes unavailable?
  12. What happens when its output is wrong?
  13. How is it contained?
  14. How is it recovered?
  15. How does it affect other systems?
  16. What evidence proves its current status?
  17. What counter-metrics measure its weaknesses?
  18. What shadow capability challenges it?
  19. Who owns its governance?
  20. What would justify removing it?

The real opportunity, as the proposal states it. Most AI security approaches concentrate on protecting a model, an API, a dataset or an application. VBYA can address something much larger: the integrity of an organization composed of autonomous and semi-autonomous agents. That includes what the organization knows; what it believes; why it believes it; who can change its beliefs; what it is allowed to do; how it coordinates; how it detects deception; how it handles disagreement; how it recognizes its own mistakes; how it recovers; how it learns without corrupting itself.

North star, as the proposal states it. An organization should never have to rely solely on the trustworthiness of the individual components it is composed of. One architectural rule is non-negotiable in the proposal: nothing gets to declare itself trustworthy. Its trust must be established through evidence outside its own claims. That applies to agents, models, tools, people, policies, receipts, deployments, Sentinel and VBYA itself. The system must be capable of questioning every layer, including the layer doing the questioning.
