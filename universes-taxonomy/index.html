The premise

This is NOT a frontier lab and NOT a competitor to one. It is a codex of Jeramie's knowledge across his verticals and interests, built to work the cognitive side of a decision: take a question, run it through everything he knows, reach a conclusion he can act on. Structure is universes, then worlds, then planets, then granular detail, like a business org chart where marketing is not with the mechanics. The end state is sovereign intelligence he can build anything off of, not an operating system.

How to read the counts. EXACT means computed from seed-index.json partition buckets. ESTIMATE means computed from a keyword rule rather than a source's own tally. UNVERIFIED means the count could not be verified from the sources read. Planet counts from keyword rules overlap, so they can sum above their World total. DESIGNED is not DEPLOYED is not LIVE, and nothing here is COMMERCIAL.

5. Shapes borrowed

Six hierarchy shapes were studied. No shape was adopted wholesale. Each was borrowed only where it fits a one-founder knowledge codex for decisions, not a staffed company org chart.

Divisional structure

Borrowed where: Top level: Worlds

Core idea: Split into semi-autonomous units by product, geography, or market, each with its own accountability; the center sets direction and holds the divisions together. Good at focus, speed inside a domain, clear ownership, easy addition of a new division. Breaks: divisions duplicate functions and stop sharing knowledge.

Fit: Strong partial fit, best candidate for the top level. The verticals map cleanly to divisions. Borrow divisions for Worlds, but add shared infrastructure and cross-cutting links so a pattern found in one World can be seen from another.

Real example: Procter and Gamble, product-based divisions (Beauty, Grooming, Health Care, Fabric and Home Care, Baby, Feminine and Family Care).

Sources: https://www.advergize.com/divisional-organizational-structure/

Platform / Ecosystem

Borrowed where: Substrate layer

Core idea: A stable core platform provides shared functionality and interfaces, and complementary apps or modules vary on top of it. Good at reuse of one stable core across many complements and compounding value as complements accumulate. Breaks: complementors depend on the platform owner and its rules; a core change can break many complements at once.

Fit: Strongest fit for the substrate layer, not for the whole taxonomy. The estate already has a natural core: governance, receipts and provenance, identity, search and retrieval, design system, shared infrastructure. The 500 items are natural complements. Keep the language honest: there is no external developer ecosystem here yet, only internal complements. Borrow the core versus complement distinction, the interface and standards discipline, and the rule that a core change must be checked against all complements.

Real example: Apple App Store on iOS.

Sources: https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/computer-science/platform-ecosystem https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12525-025-00858-9

Functional structure

Borrowed where: Tag facet, not Worlds

Core idea: Group by shared skill or function (marketing, engineering, finance, operations), one head per function. Good at deep specialization and consistent standards. Breaks: silos form, cross-functional work slows.

Fit: Weak as the primary taxonomy, useful as a secondary facet. The estate has no departments to staff; functions such as research, build, verification, distribution, and governance describe what an item does, not what it is about. Use functions as tags, not as Worlds.

Real example: Apple, one design team, one hardware engineering team, one software engineering team, one marketing team across all products.

Sources: https://www.advergize.com/functional-organizational-structure/ https://www.workhuman.com/blog/functional-organizational-structure/

Matrix structure

Borrowed where: Second index axis only

Core idea: Two axes apply at once, most often function crossed with product or project; an item sits at the intersection and is judged by both axes. Good at balancing functional depth with product focus. Breaks: dual reporting creates confusion, conflicting priorities, blurred accountability.

Fit: Useful as an indexing principle, not as a reporting structure. There are no two bosses in a one-founder codex. Borrow the matrix as a second axis only: one home World for each item, plus tags on a second axis such as lifecycle state or capability (signal, recipe, underwriting, outreach, receipt).

Real example: Philips (engineers report to a functional head and a product line); Atrium Health (regional supply chain leader reports to enterprise supply chain and regional operations).

Sources: https://www.performancereviewssoftware.com/blog/organizational-structure-types/ https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/how-to-make-your-matrix-organization-really-work/ https://www.tempo.io/blog/matrix-organizational-structure

Holding company / Conglomerate

Borrowed where: Governance edge only

Core idea: A parent exists mainly to own controlling interests in other companies; the parent allocates capital and selects leaders, while each subsidiary runs its own operations. Good at letting very different businesses run independently. Breaks: synergies are often promised and rarely delivered.

Fit: Partial fit for ownership and governance, poor fit as a taxonomy for artifacts. Most of the 500 items are not operating businesses. Borrow the holding idea only at the top governance layer, with an explicit test for what graduates from codex entry to real venture.

Real example: Berkshire Hathaway (GEICO, BNSF Railway, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, small Omaha headquarters focused on capital allocation).

Sources: https://legalclarity.org/how-is-berkshire-hathaway-structured/ https://fourweekmba.com/holding-company-organizational-structure/

Network / Franchise

Borrowed where: Repeatable template pattern only

Core idea: Distributed nodes operate under a shared brand and a shared set of rules, while a center provides the brand, the operating system, training, and supply chain. Good at fast scaling and local adaptation. Breaks: the center loses direct control, quality depends on enforcing rules across independent operators.

Fit: Weak fit as an ownership model, narrow fit as a template pattern. There are no franchisees. Borrow only the repeatable playbook idea (one well-defined format instantiated in many places, such as a scan, dossier, demo, or paper) and the brand and rules layer that keeps public surfaces consistent.

Real example: McDonald's, over 93 percent of restaurants owned by independent franchisees.

Sources: https://eathealthy365.com/the-mcdonalds-organizational-structure-explained/ https://fourweekmba.com/franchising/ https://ca.indeed.com/career-advice/career-development/what-is-virtual-organization

6. Shapes rejected

Three shapes were rejected outright.

Flat structure / holacracy

Why rejected: Rejected because at about 500 items it creates role ambiguity, unclear ownership, and coordination chaos, and named adopters such as Zappos and GitHub ran into turnover and added management layers back.

Source: https://www.workhuman.com/blog/flat-organizational-structure/

Simple structure (founder-centered, everything reports to one person)

Why rejected: Rejected because capacity is bounded by the founder's time and attention, and the founder bottleneck is the exact problem this codex is meant to reduce, not encode as the design.

Source: https://www.upistudy.com/learn/principles-of-management/traditional-structures

Bureaucratic structure (rule-driven hierarchy with many layers and written procedure for every decision)

Why rejected: Rejected because it is slow, rigid, resistant to change, and stifles creativity, which is the opposite of a working codex that must support fast founder decisions.

Source: https://www.fourweekmba.com/bureaucratic-organization/
