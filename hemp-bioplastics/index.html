Thesis

A materials platform, not a product.

Waste becomes manufacturing infrastructure. Hemp stalks, crop residues, and recovered polymers are not trash to be hauled away. They are feedstock. Formulated into composites, compounded into pellets, sheets, and preforms, and run through existing manufacturers, they become the raw material base for automotive parts, packaging, and building products. The platform owns the formulation, the supply chain, and the intelligence. The manufacturer owns the machine. The customer owns the finished product.

Section 07

Technical variables

Eight variables decide whether a composite works or fails. The interface problem underneath them is the core technical challenge of the whole platform. Click a variable row for detail, then try the loading slider.

VariableWhat it controls
Fiber lengthLonger fiber carries more load, but is harder to process evenly.
Fiber loadingHow much fiber is in the mix. More fiber, more stiffness, up to a point.
MoistureWater in the fiber causes voids, steam, and weak bonds.
Particle sizeFineness of hurd and powder fractions, which sets surface area and flow.
Matrix compatibilityWhether the chosen plastic and the fiber will bond at all.
Melt temperatureProcessing heat. Too hot degrades the fiber, too cool leaves poor mixing.
Additive chemistryCoupling agents and compatibilizers that bridge fiber and plastic.
Batch consistencyRepeatability from one production run to the next.

Section 10

Five hidden problems, and their opportunities Draft rows

Each problem is a moat in disguise. Whoever solves it first owns the lane. Click a problem to see the opportunity inside it.

Problem: fiber varies by variety, season, field, and harvest. A formulation tuned on one lot fails on the next.

Opportunity: grading, blending, and incoming quality control become a service. The platform that can certify a lot can charge for the certainty.

Problem: hemp loves water, plastics repel it, and wet fiber makes weak, void-filled parts.

Opportunity: drying discipline plus coupling-agent chemistry is proprietary know-how. It is the hardest part to copy from the outside.

Problem: there is no mature hemp fiber supply chain in the region. Growers, processors, and buyers are not yet connected.

Opportunity: the first mover that connects them becomes the toll bridge. Aggregation itself is the business.

Problem: buyers ask for test data and certifications that do not yet exist in a standard form for hemp composites.

Opportunity: building the test packs and the certification playbook first makes the platform the reference supplier.

Problem: virgin plastic is cheap and consistent. A hemp composite that costs more with no other benefit loses.

Opportunity: win on the full value: weight savings, carbon story, disposal cost, and brand value, not on resin price alone. The illustrative 100,000-pound comparison ($0.87 vs $0.91 per lb) shows the gap is closable, not closed.

Section 11

End-of-life terms: NOT interchangeable

Four terms, four different claims. Confusing them is how a product gets accused of greenwashing. Click a term to see exactly what it does and does not promise.

TermWhat it meansWhat it does NOT mean
Bio-basedMade wholly or partly from plants or other renewable biological sources.Says nothing about how it breaks down or where it goes at end of life.
BiodegradableBreaks down by biological action into water, carbon dioxide, and biomass.Says nothing about how fast, or in what conditions. A beach is not a lab.
CompostableBreaks down in composting conditions within a defined time, to a defined standard.Does not mean it will compost in a backyard pile or in a landfill.
RecyclableCan be collected, reprocessed, and made into new material in existing streams.Does not mean it will be recycled. Collection and sorting decide that.

The rule: a product can be bio-based and not biodegradable. It can be biodegradable and not compostable. It can be compostable and not recyclable. Each claim needs its own evidence, its own standard, and its own honest label.

Section 19

Closing public statement

We are building a materials platform in Michigan, from Michigan fields, for Michigan manufacturers and the buyers they serve.

Hemp is not a novelty crop and bioplastics are not a slogan. They are feedstock, formulation, and manufacturing discipline, applied to the waste streams and farm outputs this region already produces.

The goal is simple: turn what we throw away and what we grow into the materials American factories run on. Waste becomes manufacturing infrastructure. That is the work, and it starts here.
