Exact etymology

Hicks is an English patronymic surname meaning "son of Hick." Hick (or Hicke) was a common medieval rhyming nickname for Richard. Native English speakers often struggled with the Norman-French "R" sound, so "Rick/Ricard" shifted to "Hick."0

Richard itself comes from Old High German/Proto-Germanic elements:

  • rīh / rīk- → ruler, king, power, dominion
  • hart / harduz → hard, firm, brave, hardy, strong

Combined: "brave ruler," "strong in rule," or "powerful leader." The "hard/brave" element can imply the toughness needed to defend or hold territory, but it is not a direct translation of "defender." It emphasizes strength and resilience in authority rather than pure protection.30

The name exploded in popularity in England after the Norman Conquest (1066) and especially after King Richard I "the Lionheart" (1189-1199). There is no widely recognized historical figure called "Richard the Conqueror." Closest matches include:

  • Richard, second son of William the Conqueror (died young in a hunting accident)
  • Richard I of Normandy ("the Fearless"), a key ancestor in the line that produced William
  • Richard the Lionheart himself, whose military campaigns and legend made the name iconic

Many English families with Hicks (or related) surnames show medieval records in places like Gloucestershire, Yorkshire, Cornwall, and the West Country, with some later claims of knightly ties (e.g., a Sir Ellis Hicks knighted at Poitiers in 1356). Short answer: No, we do not have verified DNA samples from Richard the Lionheart, Richard I of Normandy, or William the Conqueror's son Richard.

The problem is not genealogy. The problem is authenticated biological material.

Richard the Lionheart (1157-1199)

His heart was reportedly removed, embalmed, and buried separately in Rouen.

Scientists analyzed remnants associated with the embalmed heart in 2013 and identified preservation substances such as frankincense and other compounds, which supported historical accounts of medieval embalming. However, that work was not a complete genomic identification and did not establish a usable reference DNA profile for modern genealogical matching.

Even if a full genome existed, proving descent from a specific medieval king would still be difficult because:

○ you inherit only a fraction of each ancestor's DNA over time, ○ after ~30 generations many genealogical ancestors contribute little or no detectable DNA, ○ paper genealogy and DNA genealogy become different things.

Richard I of Normandy ("The Fearless")

No authenticated DNA profile exists.

There have been studies involving Norman remains and medieval burials, but no accepted genomic reference for Richard I himself.

Richard, son of William the Conqueror

No verified DNA profile exists.

His early death and uncertain burial history make recovery and authentication even harder.

What We Do Have

What researchers can sometimes test is the male-line Y chromosome of dynasties.

The famous example is the House of Bourbon.

Living male-line descendants were compared against remains attributed to Bourbon kings.

That works because:

Father → Son → Son → Son

passes most of the Y chromosome forward.

For the Norman dynasty, the challenge is that:

○ authenticated remains are limited, ○ direct uninterrupted male lines are difficult to establish, ○ medieval remains often have contamination issues.

The Interesting Hicks Question

The more realistic question is not:

"Am I descended from Richard the Lionheart?"

The realistic question is:

"Am I descended from the same medieval Norman population that produced the Richards?"

The answer is very possibly yes if your paternal line is English with deep roots.

After 1,000 years, many millions of people with English ancestry descend from Norman nobles somewhere in their tree.

William the Conqueror alone likely has millions of living descendants today.

The surprise isn't being descended from him.

The surprise would be not being descended from him if your ancestry remained in Britain for centuries.
