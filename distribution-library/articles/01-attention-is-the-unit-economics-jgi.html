Your AI Isn't Slow. It's Expensive.

Status: DRAFT. Not published. Verification notes at the bottom.

In 2025 everybody counted agents. How many workers, how many tasks completed, how many tokens burned. All of it was the wrong scoreboard.

The right unit of measurement for an AI system is not tokens. It is founder attention. Every minute a human spends checking, correcting, approving, or rescuing an agent's output is a cost. A system that does ten tasks and needs five minutes of your supervision is worse than a system that does three tasks and needs none.

I wrote this into the design spec of my own system in 2025: maximize verified positive change in the real world per unit of operator attention. Everything else is vanity. Tasks completed means nothing if half of them were wrong. Code written means nothing if nobody deployed it. Novelty means nothing if it cost you an afternoon.

The attention shadow price

Treat your attention like it has a price, because it does. A technically cheap task becomes expensive the moment it consumes your focus. The formula that matters:

Verified value divided by founder minutes.

Run that calculation on your current agent setup honestly. Include the time you spend reading its output, fixing its mistakes, re-prompting it when it drifts, and the meetings where you explain to people what the system was supposed to do. Most "autonomous" setups in 2025 and 2026 fail this math badly. They look productive in a demo and tax you in production.

The fix is not a smarter model. It is a system designed around not needing you.

What changed since 2025

In 2025 the industry sold autonomy as a feature. "Fully autonomous" was on every landing page. By late 2026 the survivors learned the harder lesson: autonomy without verification is just faster ways to be wrong. The serious builders now talk about supervised execution with receipts. Every important machine action leaves evidence: who did it, under what authority, what the input and output were, and what happened next.

The second shift: the verification layer has to be harder than the intelligence layer. If your agent is aggressive in action but your checks are casual, you built a very fast liar. The truth machinery has to be at least as strong as the doing machinery. That means independent review, not the agent grading its own homework.

The capability ladder nobody climbs honestly

Real autonomy has levels, and almost nobody labels them correctly. A system that responds to prompts is not the same as one that discovers its own tasks. A system that executes tools is not the same as one that recovers from failure. A system that runs overnight is not the same as one that compounds learning across weeks.

Most 2025 "autonomous agents" were level 1 or 2 wearing a level 8 costume. Calling a prompt-responding chatbot "agentic" was marketing, not engineering. The honest labels: reactive, tool-using, persistent, self-starting, economic, multi-agent, self-recovering, learning, self-improving, cross-domain, organizational, long-horizon, generalist. Know where your system actually sits. Build one rung at a time.

What to do now

  1. Instrument attention. Track interventions per mission and minutes per verified outcome. If you cannot measure it, you are guessing about your own economics.
  2. Separate the doer from the checker. Whatever generates the work cannot be the only thing that verifies it. Independent review is not optional at scale.
  3. Stop optimizing token cost. Start optimizing attention cost. A model that is twice as expensive per token but never needs correction is cheaper.
  4. Demand receipts. Every consequential action should leave evidence you can audit later. If your system cannot tell you what it did and why, you do not have a system. You have a slot machine.
  5. Learn at five levels at once: what is true, how to do things reliably, which things create value, which workers should solve which problems, and how the system itself should reason and improve. Most teams only do level one.

The end state is simple to describe and hard to build: you state an outcome, the system reconstructs reality, does the work, verifies it, measures what happened, learns, and picks the next mission. You participate only where your judgment, relationships, capital, or authority are genuinely required. Everything else is the machine's job.

We are not there yet. But the builders who price attention correctly will get there first, because they are the only ones whose economics survive contact with production.
