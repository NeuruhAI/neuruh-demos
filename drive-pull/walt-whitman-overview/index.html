Who is Walt Whitman

Walt Whitman (1819-1892) was an American poet, journalist, essayist, and one of the foundational figures in American literature.

He is best known for Leaves of Grass, a poetry collection he first published himself in 1855 and kept expanding and revising for the rest of his life.

Why Whitman mattered

Whitman deliberately broke from traditional European poetic conventions. Instead of tightly structured rhyme and meter, he developed a sweeping form of free verse built around long lines, repetition, catalogues of people and places, and a conversational prophetic voice.

His central subjects were unusually ambitious:

• The individual self : not merely ego, but the self as connected to everyone else. • Democracy : the idea that ordinary workers, farmers, mechanics, sailors, women, immigrants, and marginalized people belonged inside the American story. • The human body : he treated the physical body as sacred rather than something beneath the spirit. • Nature and consciousness : nature, death, sexuality, spirituality, and identity become parts of one interconnected reality. • America itself : Whitman tried to create a distinctly American literary voice rather than imitate Europe. • Death and immortality : especially after his experiences during the Civil War.

His most famous poem, “Song of Myself,” opens with the famous idea:

“I celebrate myself, and sing myself”

But the deeper argument is not simply I am important. It is closer to:

“I am large, I contain multitudes.”

That line : from Section 51 of “Song of Myself” : is a summary of his whole project: a single person can hold contradictions, and still be part of a larger humanity.

Whitman’s life

Whitman was born on May 31, 1819, in West Hills, Long Island, New York, the second of nine children. His family moved to Brooklyn when he was four.

His early career was in journalism and printing. He worked as a printer, teacher, and newspaper editor in Brooklyn and New York City. He edited papers including the Brooklyn Daily Eagle and the Brooklyn Freeman.

During the Civil War (1861-1865), Whitman went to Washington, D.C., to find his brother George, who had been wounded. He stayed and became a volunteer nurse in military hospitals, visiting wounded soldiers : an experience that profoundly shaped his later work, especially Drum-Taps (1865) and the elegy for Abraham Lincoln, “When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom’d” (1865).

After the war, Whitman worked as a clerk in the Department of the Interior and the Attorney General’s office in Washington. He was dismissed from the Interior Department in 1865 after a superior found a copy of Leaves of Grass in his desk and considered it immoral.

In 1873, Whitman suffered a paralytic stroke. He moved to Camden, New Jersey, where he lived with his brother George and later in his own house. He continued revising Leaves of Grass until his death on March 26, 1892.

Whitman’s influence

Whitman was not widely famous in his lifetime : Leaves of Grass was controversial, praised by Emerson and others, but also condemned for its frank sexuality and democratic radicalism.

His influence after his death was enormous. He is now considered the father of American free verse, and a major influence on:

• American modernist poets • The Beat poets • Walt Whitman’s influence on American literature is comparable to Shakespeare’s on English : he created a new way of writing about ordinary American life.

Whitman also influenced:

• Democratic thought : his idea that poetry should include everyone • LGBTQ literature : his poems about male friendship and love have been read as foundational texts, although Whitman himself never publicly identified as homosexual • American identity : his vision of America as a diverse, democratic, and ever-unfinished project

What Whitman wrote beyond Leaves of Grass

• Drum-Taps (1865) : Civil War poems • Democratic Vistas (1871) : a prose essay on democracy and literature • Specimen Days (1882) : prose memoir of the Civil War and his life • November Boughs (1888) : late poems and prose

The most important idea to carry away

Whitman is not only a poet of the self. He is a poet of connection : between the body and the soul, the individual and the crowd, America and the world, life and death.

Quick clarification: Whitman should not be confused with Walt Whitman the journalist : although that is the same person : or with the later Walt Whitman Rostow, the economist, or the actor Walt Whitman. The poet is the figure discussed here.

Important distinction: Whitman’s ideas about democracy and the self are often linked to Emerson and Transcendentalism, although they need to be distinguished from claims of direct philosophical dependence.

  1. Were the Hicks a “land company”?

Not in the modern sense of a corporation called the “Hicks Land Company.” There was no single company with that name in the 17th or 18th century.

What there was: the Hicks family, as one of the early English proprietary families of Long Island, held large tracts of land through patents, purchases from the Dutch, and purchases from Native peoples, and through marriage alliances with the Seaman, Townsend, Cornell, and Mott families.

The Hicks family’s land base was centered on: • Jericho, Long Island • Westbury, Long Island • Flushing, Queens • Great Neck, Long Island • Hempstead, Long Island

By the 18th century, the Hicks family held : through direct ownership, through marriage, and through the Seaman and Townsend alliances : thousands of acres across Long Island.

  1. Elias Hicks (1748-1830) : connections and influence

Elias Hicks was born in Jericho, Long Island, on March 19, 1748. He was a Quaker minister, a farmer, a surveyor, and a carpenter.

He was not a wealthy landowner in the sense of a manor lord. He was a working farmer and preacher who traveled constantly.

But his influence was enormous:

a. Quaker influence Hicks was the leader of the “Hicksite” branch of Quakerism. After his death, American Quakers split into Hicksite and Orthodox branches : the Hicksite-Orthodox schism of 1827-1828. That split was one of the most important religious divisions in 19th-century America.

b. Abolition Hicks was an early and vocal abolitionist. He preached against slavery from the 1770s onward, and he refused to use products made by enslaved labor : sugar, cotton, and other goods.

He freed the people he had inherited as enslaved laborers, and he urged other Quakers to do the same.

c. Long Island influence Hicks was a central figure in the Jericho and Westbury Quaker meetings. He was also connected to the Seaman, Townsend, Mott, and Whitman families through marriage and meeting.

d. Whitman’s connection to Hicks Whitman admired Hicks deeply. He wrote about Hicks in Specimen Days and in his journalism. He saw Hicks as a model of the democratic, prophetic, American voice : a farmer-preacher who spoke directly, without hierarchy.

Whitman’s own father, Walter Whitman Sr., knew the Hicks family. The Whitmans were Long Island people, and the Hicks were one of the old families they knew.

Elias Hicks kept a journal from 1770s to 1830. It was published after his death as:

This is the most important source. It is available today in: • The Internet Archive (free, scanned) • Google Books • The Quaker collections at Haverford College and Swarthmore College • The New York Public Library

  1. What is the “Inner Light”?

The Inner Light is a central Quaker doctrine.

Quakers believe that every person has a direct, inward experience of God : the “Inner Light” or “Light Within” : without needing a priest, a church, or a creed.

For Hicks, the Inner Light meant: • God speaks directly to each person • No minister is needed to mediate • The Light is in everyone, including enslaved people : which is why Hicks was an abolitionist • The Light is the source of moral truth

Whitman’s idea of the self : “I celebrate myself, and sing myself” : has a parallel here. Whitman was not a Quaker, but he was deeply influenced by the Quaker idea that the divine is within the ordinary person.

  1. What is the “Inner Light” in Hicks’s own words?

Hicks wrote in his Journal about the Light Within as the guide for all moral action. He said that the Light would lead a person to refuse slavery, to refuse war, and to live simply.

  1. Consumer activism : Hicks’s boycott

Hicks was one of the earliest American advocates of a consumer boycott of slave-produced goods. He refused to use sugar and cotton produced by enslaved labor, and he urged other Quakers to do the same.

This was in the 1770s and 1780s : decades before the abolitionist boycott movement of the 1830s.
