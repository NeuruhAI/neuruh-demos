The name is the workflow

Jeramie got find-so-profit.com and asked to link the opportunity where someone sells a private car into the consumer or business market, let a dealer buy on top of retail, and build the orchestration layer and valuation on top, with AI for the technical and admin work, taking a small profit on the orchestration or advertising, and the reverse: charging dealers to advertise or buy. The assistant's answer reframed it: this is not just a car marketplace, it is an orchestration engine that sits between any asset owner and the best possible exit path. The seller does not just list; they submit to the engine. The engine scans all paths, private sale, dealer buy, auction, export, parts out, and returns a profit scenario instead of a price.

The valuation matrix

Valuation is not a single number. Find Sell Profit should produce a valuation matrix: a route, a gross value, a cost, an expected net, a time, and a confidence. The filing gives a worked example for a vehicle. Click a route to see it, and use the calculator below to test the same arithmetic on your own numbers.

Enter a gross value and a cost. The calculator returns the expected net, using the same arithmetic as the filing's worked examples. All figures here are the filing's illustrative examples, not verified market data.

Gross value: Cost: Expected net: $21,200

The filing's recommendation logic: "Dealer route is recommended because the additional expected proceeds from retail do not compensate for additional time, repair cost, and transaction risk." A $12,000 sale is not automatically superior to an $11,500 sale: if the $12,000 route takes six weeks, requires $700 of preparation, creates shipping risk, and has a 60% closing probability, while another party will pay $11,500 today, the second option may have superior utility. Profit means maximizing net economic outcome, counting fees, shipping, repairs, taxes, time-to-sale, probability of sale, fraud exposure, labor, liquidity, and more, not headline price.

Figure 2: The valuation matrix from handoff section 11. AI should never disguise uncertainty: if identification is uncertain, the system should say so and state what evidence is required, for example "Potential match: Rolex Submariner reference X. Confidence 71%. Additional caseback/serial images required."

Universal asset expansion

The concept expanded beyond automotive during the thread. Click an asset class to read what the system should do with it.

Automotive was the strongest first wedge: large transaction values, fragmented disposition channels, dealers constantly needing inventory, private-party sellers disliking the process, structured identifiers, rich comparable data, measurable dealer acquisition economics, a meaningful wholesale to private to retail spread, and dealer SaaS that can support recurring revenue. Recommended approach: launch narrow externally, build universal internally. Frontend MVP: sell your vehicle through the smartest available route. Backend schema: universal asset intelligence engine.

Figure 3: Universal asset expansion from handoff sections 4, 5, and 6, and section 24. Household discovery turns the system from a reactive valuation tool into a latent asset discovery engine: "What may I have in my house that I could potentially sell?"
Figure 4

Two-sided market and buyer graph

Find Sell Profit is a two-sided engine. The same asset graph serves both sides: a seller listing becomes structured supply, and a buyer preference becomes structured demand. The live mapping is asset to estimated value to seller objective to buyer demand to transaction channels to recommended outcome.

Dealer experience from the handoff: onboarding captures dealership information, buyer contacts, geography, makes and models, price bands, mileage bands, age, condition tolerance, title requirements, desired margins, transport radius, bid authority, and acquisition volume. Dashboards show matched vehicles, new private-party opportunities, saved searches, automated buying rules, bid history, win rate, acquisition cost, expected retail margin, local supply intelligence, and market movement. Later, autobid rules: for example, bid up to $17,800 on 2022-2024 Toyota RAV4 XLE under 50,000 miles within 150 miles if clean title and expected retail margin exceeds $2,800.

Figure 4: The two-sided market structure and buyer graph from handoff sections 7 and 8, and section 26. The buyer graph transforms buyer demand from human-readable advertisements into computable demand.
Figure 5

Single intake, multiple outcomes

One of the foundational principles: SINGLE INTAKE, MULTIPLE OUTCOMES. The user describes or uploads the asset once. The platform does not make them re-enter the same data for every transaction channel. Input may include photos, video, text, VIN, serial number, UPC, model number, reference number, address, documents, receipts, authentication paperwork, title, maintenance history, dimensions, condition description, and voice description. The engine normalizes this into a Universal Asset Record that can drive multiple workflows.

Figure 5: The seller intake principle and Universal Asset Record from handoff sections 9 and 10. The model stays category-agnostic at the core; category-specific adapters extend it.

The recipe engine

A listing alone is not an opportunity. The system should generate an executable recipe. Click a recipe to read its worked example from the filing. All figures are the filing's illustrative examples.

Over time the system learns which buyers pay more and which close faster. That becomes the defensible data loop. It is not just another marketplace; it is a layer that helps everyone make smarter buy and sell calls and takes a cut when it routes to partners.

Figure 6: The recipe engine from handoff section 14 and turn 7. Recipes are executable strategies with costs, expected proceeds, confidence, and time-to-sale, not just "here is a cheap car".

Data moat and three flywheels

The most valuable proprietary dataset is not scraped listing data alone; listing data is frequently replicable. The real proprietary dataset is intent plus valuation plus demand plus recommendation plus transaction outcome. Example from the handoff: seller would accept $18,000; dealer bid $18,400; dealer historically closes 87% of matched opportunities; retail market estimate was $21,500; seller selected immediate dealer route; actual close $18,350; time 26 hours. Over millions of transactions, this becomes extremely powerful.

Figure 9: The data moat and flywheels from handoff sections 22, 57, 58, and 59. Outcome data is the crown jewel: closed transactions, accepted bids, actual purchase prices, and actual sale prices take priority over asking prices.

Created versus not created

Section 53 of the handoff insists this distinction must remain explicit. Documentation must never be confused with implementation.

Definition of done for first commercial version

A commercial FSP v1 should not be considered complete until: a real consumer can submit a real vehicle; the vehicle can be normalized into a structured asset; FSP can calculate a defensible valuation range; at least one real qualified buyer can be matched; a real offer can be returned; multiple routes can be compared; the seller can choose one; the transaction can be completed; FSP records realized economics; the buyer sees measurable acquisition value; FSP earns or proves a repeatable path to revenue; the system requires limited manual intervention; the economics are measured; the recommendation can be audited. Until that happens, the company has a strategy and prototype, not a validated marketplace.
