00 How to read this document

This document is a forensic rebuild, not a family tree. Older Hicks material merged several different Hicks histories into one continuous line. The current record separates them, and keeps them separate until a primary record closes the gap.

Every claim in this document carries one verdict:

  • Proven Primary or corroborated documentary or genetic evidence supports it.
  • Not proven Asserted in older material, but no primary evidence closes the link.
  • Hypothesis A named candidate with supporting circumstances, awaiting a decisive record.
  • Unknown No evidence either way in the sources read.
  • Lead quality Collaborative-tree or derivative material. A lead, not a proved link.

The one rule that governs everything below. Nothing is attached above William Hicks, born about 1829-1831, until William's parentage is proved. Not Charles Renatus Hicks, not Plymouth Robert Hicks, not Trader Robert Hicks, not the medieval English pedigree, not the old mega-genealogy.

Sources for this master document are the five verbatim filings listed in Section 13. Those filings are records of ChatGPT conversations, not independently verified pedigrees. Where a filing itself reports a claim as ChatGPT synthesis, this document says so. Where the filing text was cut off in the browser view, this document does not reconstruct the missing words.

02 The brick wall: William Hicks

William Hicks, born about 1829-1831, is the brick wall. His parentage is unknown. Everything farther back depends on that answer. Three models are live: a Bucks County, Pennsylvania Quaker Hicks family (serious candidate); an unknown Ohio/Indiana Hicks family (still completely viable); direct descent from Charles Renatus / Cherokee Hicks (not proved and cannot presently be attached). The documentary geography currently points more toward a Pennsylvania to Ohio/Indiana migration than toward Georgia / Cherokee Nation.

2.1 Penrose Hicks: the strongest named candidate, unproved

Penrose Hicks, born 9 May 1802 in Milford Township, Pennsylvania, was a birthright member of Richland Monthly Meeting (Quaker) and married Mary Ball in 1828. A Bucks County biographical source described Penrose's father as George Hicks and his mother as Ann Penrose. Penrose and Mary Ball reportedly had "a number of children" whose identities were not all supplied in the sketch. Hypothesis

Why Penrose is compelling: correct generation, correct surname, right age to father a child around 1829-1831, Bucks County / Pennsylvania geography, documented children whose full names are not supplied in the secondary biography, and a Quaker record infrastructure capable of documenting births and removals.

Why it is not proved: Penrose to William remains unproved. The kill-shot records are still outstanding:

  1. Penrose Hicks probate, Bucks County, 1886. If it names a son William, the hypothesis becomes a proved link. If it does not, the Penrose theory weakens substantially.
  2. Richland Monthly Meeting birth and removal records.
  3. 1850 census, Penrose household.
  4. 1850-1870 census, William Hicks migration trail.
  5. Any Quaker certificate of removal mentioning William.

Under the Penrose hypothesis, the earlier framework would be George Hicks married Ann Penrose on 4th month, 4th day, 1799, in a Gwynedd-area Quaker register, with earlier Hicks marriages in the same source environment including William Hicks and Hannah Shaw (1760) and Jesse Hicks and Mary Ball (1785). That establishes a substantial Hicks population in the southeastern Pennsylvania Quaker network. It does not yet prove which earlier William or Jesse belongs in the direct paternal chain. The first three generations of that diagram are research architecture, not a proved direct pedigree. Not proven

03 The Y-DNA framework

The direct paternal Y-line is I-M253 to I-L22, an old northern-European paternal lineage strongly associated with Scandinavia and North Sea populations. The working terminal branch is I-FTD69337, reached through the ladder below.

What I-M253 to I-L22 is. Proven The direct paternal Y-line is I-M253 to I-L22. I2a is a different branch (I-M438 to I2a, common in the Balkans, Sardinia, and eastern Europe) and is not this line. L2a is mitochondrial DNA, not Y-DNA, and is not this line. There is no evidence yet that I-FTD69337 descends through P109 to FGC21810; that would be a different branch.

05 Cherokee Hicks: historically real, not connected to this line

Charles Renatus Hicks (1767-1827) is historically real and important. He is NOT connected to the documentary spine above William Hicks. The missing paper bridge proving that William Hicks descended from the Cherokee Hicks family does not exist in the sources read. The two Hicks systems may converge. The proof must occur at or immediately above William Hicks, c. 1829-1831.

5.1 Charles Renatus Hicks

  • Born 23 December 1767 at Tomotley / Tomatly on the Hiwassee, son of trader Nathan Hicks and a Cherokee mother. Died 20 January 1827.
  • Baptized into the Moravian faith in 1813 and acquired the name "Renatus". Interpreter, political leader, Cherokee treasurer, Second / Deputy Principal Chief, de facto political leadership during Pathkiller's later years, Principal Chief in January 1827.
  • Four letters to John Ross, 1825-1826, preserved in the John Howard Payne Papers, Newberry Library, Box 7, Folder 1, also published in the University of Nebraska Press edition of the Payne-Butrick papers. The letters were produced at Ross's request as Hicks tried to preserve Cherokee history and traditions before removal. One portion reconstructs Cherokee settlement around the mouth of the Hiwassee River, with Yuchi (Uchees) as nearest neighbours on the south side, Creek settlement at Sawtee above the mouth of Ooltewah Creek, and "no account of any hostile nature" between these populations during that period.
  • "Manners and Customs of the Cherokees", 13 October 1818, drawn up by Charles Hicks, requested by missionary Ard Hoyt, copied from the mission diary, originally in Hicks's own handwriting, recorded "nearly in his own words". It describes seven clans, marriage within one's own clan prohibited, children belonging to the clan of the mother, clan land held in common with the poorest men holding the same right as the greatest, and the Green Corn ritual lasting four days, including a ritual language partly lost even in 1818.
  • His library: widely repeated claims that it was among the largest, or the largest, personal libraries in North America are not yet primary-source verified. No contemporaneous inventory or original documentary statement proving the ranking has been found. Confirmed: unusually literate, read extensively, wrote historical and cultural material, participated in Cherokee government. Not proven (ranking claim).
  • Sequoyah as cousin of Charles, Charles helping Sequoyah learn or sign letters, Charles materially contributing to syllabary development: assertions in older material, treated as hypotheses requiring primary evidence, not established genealogy. Hypothesis

07 Long Island, the Quakers, and the two Hicksvilles

The Long Island Hicks network is a commercial-Quaker kinship network, and it is the strongest Hicks network in the record after the documentary spine itself. It is also the network that produced the Hicksite schism.

7.1 Elias Hicks and the Hicksite split

  • Elias Hicks was a Quaker minister whose theology placed direct inward revelation above Scripture, doctrine, and institution. His hierarchy of authority was approximately: Divine Spirit / Truth, direct inward revelation, human conscience and transformation, Scripture as testimony to revelation, religious doctrine / institution. Not Bible to doctrine to authority to believer.
  • In 1827-28, American Quakers split into Hicksite and Orthodox branches. The Hicksite branch followed Elias Hicks and emphasized the Inner Light. The term "Hicksism" appears in historical indexes of the schism. Philadelphia alone had roughly 24,000 Friends involved in the institutional conflict.
  • Elias Hicks was also a surveyor, and the Elias Hicks Manuscript Collection in the Friends Historical Library contains correspondence, sermons, family correspondence, and surveyor's drawings. His Journal of the Life and Religious Labours (1832 edition, digitized by the Library of Congress and on Project Gutenberg) and his Letters (1834) are the primary sources. A modern thematic reconstruction organizes a major section under "Mystic" and documents visions including a "great ball of fire" and a rainbow vision.
  • Elias distrusted railroads. His son-in-law Valentine Hicks then became deeply involved in the Long Island Rail Road shortly after Elias died, named in the 1834 charter. The contrast is recorded as ironic, not as a single Hicks ideology. The network has internal polarity.
  • Elias to Whitman is an investigative chain, not a proven single causal chain: George Fox / early Quaker mysticism, Quietism, Elias Hicks, Hicksite Quakerism, Lucretia Mott and radical reform, abolition / women's rights / religious liberalism, Whitman, American religious universalism. Walt Whitman knew Hicks's world, described him as a combination of mystic and logical reasoner, and left an essay titled Notes (Such as They Are) Founded on Elias Hicks.
  • Whether the Hicksite ancestors of this line were a factor in American religious rights, and whether Hicks contributed to an American lineage of religious universalism that later fed Transcendentalism, are hypotheses worth investigating intellectually, not proved causal chains. Hypothesis

7.3 Hicksville, Ohio: the same extended network

Hicksville NY and Hicksville OH are one extended Long Island / New York Hicks commercial-Quaker network. This is a documented economic network, not a surname coincidence.

The 1883 History of Defiance County, Ohio says the Hicks Land Company controlled roughly 14,000 of Hicksville Township's 23,040 acres. Its lands were entered in 1835-36 in the names of Henry W. Hicks and Isaac S. Smith. Henry was identified as a member of Samuel Hicks and Sons, shipping merchants at 80 South Street, New York. Their agent Alfred P. Edgerton then laid out and developed Hicksville, built roads and mills, and the Ohio History Connection catalogs letter books, cashbooks, and warranty deeds relating to Edgerton's work as agent. The Ohio village's own institutional history explicitly associates Hicksville, New York with the same business / family network.

The chain: Elias Hicks religious network, to the Hicks / Seaman marriage network, to the Isaac / Samuel / Valentine Hicks mercantile network, to Samuel Hicks and Sons, to Henry W. Hicks, to New York shipping capital, to western land acquisition, to Hicksville, Ohio. Samuel brought sons John and Henry into partnership in 1825; the firm operated at 80 South Street until Samuel's death in 1837. Separate research on antebellum Quaker merchants identifies Samuel, John H., and Henry W. Hicks as related to Elias Hicks. Hicksville NY: railroad plus real-estate development. Hicksville OH: shipping capital plus western land speculation plus town building. The infrastructure progression is merchant house to ships to land to roads to towns to railroads.

No evidence currently ties the Hicks Land Company or its Hicks founders to William / Cyrus. It is a legitimate Hicks historical research target, not a family connection yet. Not proven

Two further lanes are open, not claimed: Hicksford, Virginia (later part of Emporia), confirmed as a place, family connection unresolved; and Upper Canada, where the Hicksite schism propagated into Canadian Friends and Elias's biography records Canadian travel and correspondence, but household-level removal certificates and marriage records are still needed before saying which specific households connect. Unknown

08 Nevis: Hicks Village and Hicks Estate

Hick's Village is on Nevis, in Saint Kitts and Nevis, in the eastern Caribbean, not in the Turks and Caicos. It sits in Saint James Windward Parish, in the northeastern part of Nevis, near Hick's Estate, Hick's Cove, Brick Kiln, Mannings, and Newcastle, around 17.184 degrees N, 62.560 degrees W. Modern geographic records explicitly list "Hick's Village" as an alternate name.

8.1 Hicks Estate (Clay Hill and the Spring)

  • A 1906 Hicks Estate plan survives in the Eastern Caribbean Court House records, British Library Endangered Archives Programme, reference EAP794/1/10/1/133, one TIFF image. Proven
  • The estate is identified as Hicks Estate, "Clay Hill and the Spring", Saint James Windward, Nevis. A compilation of Nevis plantation records from 1817 lists Hicks among the estates in Saint James Windward. In 1833, the Hicks estate school had 32 pupils, children associated with the enslaved population of the estate system. Britain abolished slavery in its Caribbean colonies in 1834, with full freedom after the apprenticeship period in 1838.
  • Richard Lythcot Hicks, a Nevis planter, is associated with the Hicks / Clay Hill estate. He married Frances Mary Jefferys. He left Nevis for America later in 1810 and died in America in 1811. Frances Mary Hicks was leasing Clay Hill and Spring plantations in 1851 to John and William Huggins.
  • Richard Lythcot Hicks Jr., born shortly before 1811, married Georgina Eliza Huggins, granddaughter of Edward Huggins. Georgina died in August 1835, age 21. Richard Jr. died five months later, age 26. Their memorials are at St. George's Church, Gingerland, Nevis. The Henry Moore Foundation catalogue of the sculptor Matthew Wharton records a Richard Hicks funerary monument (died 1836, St George, Gingerland) and a Georgiana Hicks funerary monument (died 1835).
  • In the 1870s, Henry Wilkin was a tenant on Hicks Estate, and in 1875 he petitioned for its sale. The Wilkin family acquired Hicks Estate around 1874 through the West Indian Incumbered Estates process.
  • Archaeological field investigation by David Small and David Rollinson reported a windmill of uncertain date possibly associated with Hope, a possible animal mill at Hicks, springs supplying water, and plantation infrastructure in the valley shared by Hope / Spring Valley, Hicks / Clay Hill / Spring, New River, Butlers, and Newcastle.
  • Hicks and Hickman are NOT automatically the same thing. Hickman's / Hickman's Village in Gingerland and the Hickman plantation are separate nodes. No automatic merging.
  • The 1871 John Alexander Burke Iles Map of the Island of Nevis is the first highly detailed published map of Nevis showing estate and settlement detail. CaribMap holds a historical map archive with Nevis maps from 1690 through 1996.

8.2 Claim 103: the 1836 compensation record

Claim 103 is a HYPOTHESIS, not proven. The British parliamentary Slave Compensation Claims list for Nevis contains Claim 103: Richard L. Hicks, 204 enslaved people, 3,504 pounds 0 shillings 1 penny, award dated in the 1836 compensation period. The identification of Richard L. Hicks in Claim 103 as Richard Lythcot Hicks Jr. is very plausible because of the dates (Jr. died in late 1835, awards were processed in 1836), but it is not proven without the underlying claim file. Likewise, that the 204 people were the Hicks Estate / Hicks-Clay Hill population is likely, but needs the claim's estate / property field. The claim file must establish the exact claimant, estate name, trustee or executor, estate location, whether it was Hicks Estate, whether the 204 people were at Hicks, and the relationship between Richard L. Hicks and the estate.

No connection is claimed between the Nevis Hicks family and the documentary spine above William Hicks. The Nevis line is a separate Hicks history, recorded here because the place name and estate are real and because the compensation record is the single most important next document in that lane. The British colonial slave registers for Nevis (1817, 1822, then every three years through 1834, UK National Archives series T 71/364-369) and the parish registers are the record sets that could reconstruct the estate population before and after emancipation. Hicks remains a living Nevis surname; the 2025 St Kitts and Nevis telephone directory contains multiple Hicks entries on Nevis, including people associated with Newcastle and Whitehall. That does not prove descent from Richard Lythcot Hicks.

09 Denisovan DNA: no paternal connection

Denisovans were first a genetic discovery, not a fossil discovery. DNA sequenced from a finger bone found in Denisova Cave in the Altai Mountains of southern Siberia, Russia, did not match Neanderthals or modern humans. The finger bone is roughly 30,000-50,000 years old. Its mtDNA differed from modern humans at 385 nucleotide positions, nearly twice the number for Neanderthal mtDNA. A Denisovan molar from the same cave is huge and morphologically distinct. In 2019, a mandible from Baishiya Karst Cave in Tibet was identified as Denisovan from protein analysis.

There is no Denisovan paternal connection. Denisovan ancestry is autosomal, not Y-chromosomal. There is no Denisovan Y-haplogroup, and the paternal line I-M253 to I-L22 is a northern-European Y lineage. No autosomal Denisovan percentage for Jeramie Hicks was established in the sources read for this document. Any Denisovan connection would have to be tested autosomally, and no such result is in the record.

The Moravian record. The Moravians (Unitas Fratrum / Unity of the Brethren, renewed at Herrnhut under Count Nikolaus Ludwig von Zinzendorf) were obsessive record keepers. The Cherokee Moravian mission began at Springplace on 13 July 1801 (Abraham Steiner and Gottlieb Byhan). The Moravian Archives series runs chronologically; volumes 3 through 7 cover 1805-1827, including Charles Hicks's conversion era, Cherokee vocabulary and schooling, the syllabary and New Echota era, and the deaths of Charles Hicks, Pathkiller, and John Gambold. Anna Rosina Gambold, educator at the Bethlehem Female Seminary, diarist and botanist, is a key node: Cherokee botanical knowledge to European scientific classification to American science. The Moravians must be read critically: agents of Christianization and accidental preservationists of pre-Christian Cherokee culture at the same time.

James Mooney. Myths of the Cherokee was built from field seasons 1887-1890 among Cherokee communities, including history, archaeology, place-names, personal names, botany, medicine, arts, home life, religion, songs, ceremonies, and language, plus original Cherokee manuscripts. The James Mooney collection (bulk 1887-1921) survives at the Smithsonian's National Anthropological Archives, including material from Ayunini / Swimmer, Tilalilugi, and Consini. Mooney's Sacred Formulas of the Cherokees reports roughly 600 formulas collected in 1887-88. The Wahnenauhi manuscript (Historical Sketches of the Cherokees) is an independent Cherokee-authored voice; portions were quoted by Mooney, so provenance must be checked per claim.
