The Robot Question Is Insurance: What I Wrote in 2025, and What 2026 Did About It

Book 5 of The Neuruh Papers. Not legal advice, not insurance advice, just engineering and business analysis from a year of watching the receipts land.

In 2025 I wrote an internal working paper called "The Liability Layer: Engineering Safety for the Physical Economy." The argument was unfashionable. While the whole industry argued about which AI model was smartest, I argued that intelligence would not be the thing that gates robots entering the economy. Liability would. When the robot crashes, who pays? And how do we prove what happened?

This essay is the rest of that story: what 2026 did to the paper, what it got right, what I got wrong, and what it means for anyone running a business anywhere near physical AI.

Bits to kilograms

For about three years we obsessed over syntax. Prompt engineering. Coaxing the chatbot into writing the better email. That phase ended, not with a press release but with a quiet shift in where the interesting problems live. AI crossed from moving pixels to moving atoms. I call it the Great Materialization.

Here is the difference in plain terms. A chatbot hallucinates in the cloud and a customer support email has a made-up policy number in it. Embarrassing, fixable, contained. A vision model hallucinates on a warehouse floor and a 200-kilogram humanoid takes a step it should not have taken. That is a workers comp claim, an OSHA investigation, and a lawsuit. Same root failure: hallucination. Different physics. One is a typo. One is a tort.

And here is why liability gates adoption instead of capability. Enterprise buyers are not buying intelligence. They are buying lower labor cost or higher throughput. Both of those vanish the first time an uninsured incident hits the balance sheet. Engineers ask, "Does it work?" The CFO asks, "Who pays when it breaks, and how much of our upside does that answer eat?" The industry spent 2025 answering the engineer. In 2026, the CFO started asking. The carriers, the regulators, and the courts answered first.

The 2026 scorecard

Every number below carries an audit date. Markets move. Verify before you bet money on any of this.

Through the first half of 2026, more than sixty property and casualty insurance groups filed AI exclusions with state regulators. Audit date: July 2026. In April 2026, regulators approved over 80 percent of the AI exclusion filings from subsidiaries of Berkshire Hathaway, Chubb, Travelers, and AIG, letting the largest corporate insurers carve AI-related claims out of standard liability policies.

Read that twice. The companies whose entire business is pricing risk looked at AI, said "we cannot price this," and walked away. When my paper said liability was the bottleneck, the carriers agreed by exiting.

On September 8, 2026, Hangzhou passed the first dedicated law for embodied-intelligence robots anywhere in the world: liability frameworks for robot-caused accidents, insurance requirements for industrial deployments, mandated human oversight ratios. Shenzhen, Shanghai, Beijing, and Wuhan announced similar programs within 48 hours. In Europe, the new product liability directive expressly includes software, including AI systems, in the definition of "product." In the US, a congressional proposal would require AI companies to carry extensive liability insurance before releasing models. The regulatory direction is consistent across jurisdictions. Someone is going to be liable, and the answer increasingly points at the companies that build and deploy the systems.

So the thesis held. Now the part I owe you.

What the paper got wrong

First, the timeline. China's 2026 robot deployment ran ahead of governance, not behind it. One manufacturer filed for a public listing in July 2026 at a valuation above 60 billion yuan, reporting industrial robot sales at 34 percent of first-half revenue, up from 8 percent for all of 2025. The paper assumed regulation would arrive before scale. In China, scale arrived first and the law sprinted to catch up. That is a warning, not a contradiction: deployed fleets without a liability layer are a claim waiting for a date.

Second, the paper underweighted fleet correlation. It treated each robot's risk as roughly independent. 2026 showed that a fleet running the same model has the same blind spots everywhere at once. One bad software update can degrade a thousand robots in an hour. As of September 2026 there is no catastrophe model for correlated AI failure, no historical loss data to calibrate one, and standard insurance math assumes independence. Correlation breaks it.

Third, I was optimistic about a neutral "DMV for robots" forming. Say it plainly: no company operates as a neutral licensing authority for robots. The specialist AI liability insurers that exist are real but small, against an economy of physical machines. The gap the paper identified is still open, which is either an indictment of the thesis or a market entry sign, depending on your temperament.

And one more piece of honesty, which I will attach to this idea every time it appears: the mechanism I proposed in the paper, the Insured Action Protocol, where every robotic action pays a micro-premium and earns a signed permit before the motors unlock, is a design proposal. No one has built it. No insurer has underwritten it. No regulator has blessed it. A blueprint is not a building, and I will not let the distinction blur.

The idea in plain words

Strip a modern robot down and the industry has built two of three layers. The muscle: actuators, sensors, mechatronics, solved engineering with a roadmap. The brain: vision-language-action models and planning systems, improving fast and soaking up most of the capital. The third layer, the conscience, does not exist as infrastructure. That is the layer that decides whether a planned action is permitted. Not whether it is smart. Whether it is allowed, who authorized it, what the risk was at authorization time, and where the evidence lives afterward. Hope is not an architecture.

The discipline I proposed has four moves. Each one is simple to say and hard to build, which is why the industry has built none of them as shared infrastructure.

One: separate the planner from the authorizer. The system that wants to move is never the system that permits the move. The motors stay locked until the authorization lands. The analogy is aviation. The pilot flies the plane, but air traffic control clears the route, and nobody considers that an insult to the pilot's intelligence.

Two: score every action for risk before it happens. Speed, force, proximity to humans, the model's own uncertainty about its perception. A number, computed the same way every time, logged with the action. An underwriter cannot price a fleet, but it can price a distribution of scored actions. Ten million actions at known risk scores is an actuarial object. Ten million unscored actions is a prayer.

Three: make insurance the execution gate. The action pays a micro-premium into a risk pool, receives a signed permit, and only then do the motors unlock. No permit, no motion. Annual policies price the average, and the average lies, because a fleet's risk concentrates in specific actions, specific shifts, and specific software versions. Per-action pricing forces the true cost of risky behavior into the moment of decision, which is the only place it can change behavior.

Four: keep an append-only record. Every proposal, every risk score, every premium payment, every permit, every outcome, written where nobody can rewrite it after the fact. When something goes wrong, the question "what happened" has one authoritative answer instead of three vendors pointing at each other. It turns a litigation strategy into a reading exercise.

The part that closes deals

Here is the most commercially actionable idea in the book, and it has nothing to do with robots being smart. Novel governance is a sales objection. Boring, recognized compliance is a sales accelerator.

Enterprise deals die in risk review more often than they die in technical evaluation. A normal six-month procurement cycle is often four weeks of technical validation and twenty weeks of security, legal, and risk review. Every question your package answers in advance is weeks off the cycle. Every novel framework you ask the buyer to learn is weeks onto it.

So anchor to frameworks the auditors already trust: NIST, SOC 2, the AI risk management framework, the emerging robot safety standards. Walk into the room with a control mapping, a sample incident record showing exactly what the buyer's risk committee would receive after an event, and your insurance structure with the honest answer about what is excluded. Notice what is not on that list: a single claim about how smart the model is. The model conversation happened in the technical evaluation. The compliance package is what gets the CFO's signature, and the CFO's signature is what gets the fleet deployed.

One caveat, because this series does not sell fairy tales. Compliance is not safety, and sophisticated buyers know it. The honest pitch says it out loud: "Our compliance package proves we run the controls we claim to run. Our authorization records prove what the fleet actually did." That honesty is itself a sales accelerator.

What it means in 2026

Three things, if you run a business anywhere near this.

First, if you deploy physical AI, write the liability allocation before the robot enters the facility, and confirm coverage in writing. As of 2026, existing commercial policies often exclude AI-caused physical losses, and the exclusions are spreading. If your carrier will not confirm coverage, you are self-insuring. Price the deployment accordingly.

Second, build the separation early. Even if your authorizer starts as a simple rules engine and your permits start as logged approvals, put the split between the planner and the authorizer into the architecture while the fleet is small. Retrofitting it later is a rewrite. Start the append-only record on day one. Storage is cheap. Reconstruction is expensive.

Third, watch the correlation problem. Know how many of your units share the same model backbone, demand staged rollouts with rollback rights in your vendor contracts, and ask your broker what happens to your coverage in a correlated event. Do not accept a vague answer. The update that skips the gate is the incident that defines your year.

The future of AI is not about who has the smartest chatbot. It is about who has the safest hands, and who can prove it. That sentence closed the 2025 paper, and a year of evidence made it more true, not less.

The Insured Action Protocol is still a blueprint. The DMV for robots is still an argument. But every forcing function points the same way: exclusions pushing risk back onto deployers, regulations requiring insurance and records, buyers demanding proof. The operators who put the receipt layer in before they scale the capability layer, who separate the planner from the authorizer while the system is small, will be the only ones who can get through procurement, survive the first incident, and keep deploying while everyone else is in discovery with their carriers.

Safest hands win. Prove it.
