I Priced My Own Attention Like a Compute Budget

Most founders track their money. Almost none of them price their attention.

I do now. One line in a spec I wrote for my own AI system changed how I run my week:

"Optimize: VerifiedValue / FounderMinute."

That is the whole game. Every meeting, every context switch, every "quick question," every hour I spend doing something a machine could do with a receipt attached. It all gets priced the way an inference engine prices a tool call. If the verified value per minute of my attention is low, the task dies or gets delegated. No sentimentality about it.

Here is why this matters more than any productivity hack you have read this year.

Attention is the only truly non-renewable resource in the building

Compute gets cheaper every quarter. Models get faster. Storage is basically free. My attention is the one input that does not scale, does not get a price cut, and does not come back. A technically cheap task can be ruinously expensive if it eats founder minutes. I have watched myself burn an afternoon on a $40 problem because it was "quick." That is not being hands-on. That is mispricing the scarcest asset in the company.

So I built the rule into the machine itself. In the JGI spec, section 12, there is a formal concept: the Jeramie Attention Shadow Price. It gets estimated from real data. How often was I interrupted. How long did it take. What did it cost in opportunity. Whether the outcome justified the tap on my shoulder. Whether the interruption could have been avoided entirely.

The system is not allowed to treat my attention as free. Neither am I.

The attention firewall

The spec has a section called the Attention Firewall, and it is ruthless. I am interrupted only when one of these is true:

Authority is required. A genuine business preference is needed. Credentials or 2FA are involved. Something destructive needs recovery. A contradiction cannot be resolved without me. A large strategic fork needs founder judgment.

Otherwise: solve it.

That last line is doing the heavy lifting. "Otherwise: solve it." Most of what lands on a founder's desk does not clear that bar. It gets answered anyway, because founders confuse being needed with being useful. I built a system that refuses to flatter me that way. If the machine can resolve it, verify it, and leave a receipt, my phone stays silent and the work still happens.

There is a companion rule I like even more: "The organism never interprets silence from the founder as nothing to do." No news from me is not a pause button. It is a green light to keep discovering work, keep scoring it, keep executing what clears the authority bar, and queue the rest for my review.

No status theater

Section 89 is titled "No Status Theater" and it is two sentences long: "Do not report: 'I am working.' Report transitions. Silence otherwise."

I have sat in rooms where grown adults spent forty-five minutes narrating work that had not changed since the last meeting. That is attention priced at zero. In my system, work reports only when its state actually changes: claimed, checkpointed, done, blocked, needs authority, passed verification. Everything else is silence, and silence is the sound of attention being preserved.

Try running your week that way. No "just checking in." No meeting that exists to prove the meeting was worth having. State changes or it did not happen.

The bottleneck rule

Section 179 of the spec is bottleneck theory, one paragraph: identify the current constraint on throughput, and "optimizing non-bottlenecks may create no throughput improvement."

This is where founders lie to themselves most. We polish the website when the bottleneck is sales calls. We reorganize the file system when the bottleneck is that nobody is closing. We build the dashboard when the bottleneck is that nobody is looking at the dashboard. Polishing a non-bottleneck feels like work and prices like leisure, because the attention spent there buys zero additional throughput.

The shadow price makes the lie visible. If my attention is the constraint, then every minute I spend on a non-bottleneck has a negative return. Not zero. Negative. Because that minute was stolen from the bottleneck.

What survived the cut

Once you price attention honestly, the week reorganizes itself.

Things that survived: closing calls, because revenue per founder-minute is the highest number in the building. Writing the actual hard thing only I can write, because nobody else has my twenty-five years of pattern recognition in overlooked assets. Decisions with real forks in them, because judgment is the one thing that cannot be compiled. And rest, because a depleted founder prices every subsequent minute badly.

Things that died: status meetings, most email, anything I was doing because it felt productive, and every task where the verified outcome was smaller than the attention receipt.

The metric

I will leave you with the formula, because formulas survive where motivation does not:

VerifiedValue / FounderMinute.

Not hours worked. Not tasks completed. Not lines of code or posts published or meetings attended. Verified value, divided by the minutes of founder attention it consumed. If that ratio is climbing, the company is getting smarter. If it is falling, you are busy and going backwards.

Price yourself like you price your servers. You are the most expensive compute in the building. Act like it.
