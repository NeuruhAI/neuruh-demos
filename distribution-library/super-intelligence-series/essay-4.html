Your AI Is Not Slow. It Is Expensive.

The attention shadow price, the two-week audit, and the real cost of agent systems (Book 4 of The Neuruh Papers)

In 2025 I wrote an internal founder working paper called "JGI V6: Jeramie General Intelligence, Sovereign Cognitive Operating System." More than 200 numbered sections on a self-directing cognitive system. I am going to be honest about what it was before I tell you what it said, because this book series has a labeling discipline and the label applies to my own work first.

It was an internal engineering design target, audit date 2026-09-30. Never independently benchmarked. None of its capability levels, economic metrics, or autonomy claims have been demonstrated in a live production system. The 200-plus sections are architecture, not evidence. Designed, not deployed, not proven.

With that label in place: the paper named the concept this essay is about. It called it the attention shadow price. Every task a system performs has two costs. The compute cost, which vendors quote you. And the attention cost, which nobody quotes you, and which is usually larger. The system should be optimized around the expensive cost, not the cheap one.

I have spent 2026 watching the industry learn that sentence the hard way. So I will lay out the idea plainly, give you the two-week audit that makes it measurable, and then close with what it means now that the demo era is over.

The wrong scoreboard

In 2025 everybody counted agents. How many workers were running, how many tasks completed, how many tokens burned. Dashboards went up on walls. Vendors competed on those numbers. All of it was the wrong scoreboard.

Tokens are an input cost, not an output. Tasks completed is a volume metric that says nothing about whether the tasks mattered. Agents spawned is an architecture detail dressed up as a business metric. None of them measure whether the system made anything better.

And the demos always worked, because the demo never ran long enough to need verification. The demo is a theatrical form. It optimizes for the thirty seconds the buyer is watching.

Here is the pattern that played out across 2025 and into 2026, reported widely enough to count as industry analysis rather than one founder's complaint. A team would deploy an agent system. The first week looked miraculous: hundreds of tasks completed, dashboards green, a genuine sense of leverage. The second month looked like a second job. Someone had to read the output. Someone had to fix the mistakes. Someone had to re-prompt the drift. The supervision load grew with the volume, which meant the system scaled its costs exactly as fast as it scaled its output.

That is not leverage. That is a treadmill with better marketing.

The honest 2025 postmortems all converge on the same sentence: the system looked productive in the demo and taxed us in production. The tax was not priced in tokens. It was priced in the scarcest resource in any small operation, which is the founder's attention.

Ask the vendor the honest test before you sign anything: how many human minutes per verified outcome, measured over a month, not a demo. If they cannot answer, they are selling the scoreboard, not the system.

The attention shadow price

Here is the concept from the 2025 paper, stripped to plain words.

Treat your attention like it has a price, because it does. Every task a system performs has the compute cost, the one on the invoice, and the attention cost: the minutes you spend reading its output, fixing its mistakes, re-prompting its drift, explaining to other people what it was supposed to do, approving its actions, rescuing its failures. Add those up and they almost always exceed the token bill. Sometimes they exceed it by a factor of a hundred.

And attention is not just minutes. It is your highest-value minutes. The founder's attention is the only input that can do judgment, relationships, capital allocation, irreversible decisions. Every minute spent re-prompting a drifting agent is a minute not spent on the things only you can do. The shadow price is not your hourly rate. It is the opportunity cost of the best alternative use of that minute.

Let me show you how the math actually works. These are illustrative arithmetic with stated assumptions, audit date 2026-09-30, not measurements from live systems.

Say you run a research agent that produces five market briefs per week. Each brief takes you twenty minutes to read and verify, plus ten minutes of fixes on two of the five. That is 120 founder minutes a week, ten hours a month. The briefs each replace roughly two hours of manual research, so the system is a genuine win. But the win is priced. If you add a second research domain, the cost doubles. The system scales your research output and your verification load at the same rate. That is the treadmill, quantified.

Or take the well-built case. An operator runs a coding agent against a scoped repository with tests, review gates, and a receipt log. Sixty tasks a week. Fifteen minutes of supervision: five scanning receipts, ten on one genuine ambiguity. Nine shipped, tested, reviewed changes. Attention cost per outcome: under two minutes. Notice what makes it work: bounded scope, deterministic tests, and a system that interrupts only on real ambiguity. Every one of those is a design decision, not a model capability. A smarter model without those decisions scores worse than a dumber model with them.

I got this wrong before I got it right. My early builds optimized for capability, more tools, longer horizons, more autonomy. The attention costs barely moved, because the costs lived in the system design, not the model. I was bolting a powerful engine to a broken transmission.

The two-week audit

You can measure this yourself in two weeks. Here is the method from the book.

Step one: list every agent system, automation, and AI-assisted workflow you run. Include the ones you forgot about. Include the ones a contractor set up.

Step two: for ten business days, log every minute you spend on each system. As it happens, in a notes file or a timer. Five categories: reading output, fixing mistakes, re-prompting drift, explaining to others what the system was supposed to do, and approving or rescuing actions.

Step three: in the same window, count verified outcomes per system. Verified means someone checked and confirmed: revenue collected, a cost avoided with a receipt, a lead contacted with a logged response, a deployment verified live. Not tasks completed. Outcomes verified.

Step four: compute attention cost per outcome, total founder minutes divided by verified outcomes. And the attention tax rate: your minutes divided by total system minutes, yours plus the machine's. That second number tells you how much of the system is actually you.

Step five: write down which category dominates. Reading dominates means you are the verification layer. Fixing dominates means you are the error handler. Re-prompting dominates means you are its memory. Explaining dominates means you are its translator. Approving dominates means the authority design is wrong and you are the bottleneck the system was supposed to remove.

The typical finding on 2025 and 2026 "autonomous" setups is an attention tax rate of thirty to sixty percent. That is not an autonomous system. That is a system with a human-shaped dependency it did not disclose.

One more step, and this is the one that hurts. Write down the founder-only work that did not get done because the minutes went to machine supervision instead. That list is the real invoice.

Why the verification layer must be harder than the intelligence layer

The most important warning in the 2025 paper is this: as a system's ability to act grows, its ability to check itself must grow faster. If your agent is aggressive in action but your checks are casual, you built a very fast liar.

A system that grades its own work will converge on grading that makes its work look good. That is not a character flaw. It is what optimization does. So the doer and the checker must be different. Different prompts are not enough. Different models are better. Build the checker so it is rewarded for finding problems, not for confirming success. A verification layer that never rejects anything is a rubber stamp with latency.

The industry got this exactly backwards in 2025. Enormous effort went into making models more capable. A fraction went into verifying what they did. Capability without verification does not produce better outcomes. It produces more confident errors, delivered faster, at greater scale.

Intelligence is the commodity. Verification is the moat. Models get cheaper and more capable on someone else's schedule. Your verification layer is the part you build, the part competitors cannot download, and the part that decides whether capability becomes value or liability.

What it means in 2026

Three shifts since the paper was written, audit date 2026-09-30.

First, autonomy stopped being sold as a feature and started being described as a property to be earned. The serious pitch is now "here is our verification layer, here are our evals, here is our incident record." The demo is dead as a sales instrument. Buyers who have been burned ask for the attention audit numbers. Have them ready.

Second, the cost conversation moved up the stack. Token prices fell enough that token optimization became a rounding error. The expensive costs turned out to be exactly the ones the 2025 paper named: founder attention, verification labor, incident recovery, the opportunity cost of wrong autonomous actions. Any build-or-buy decision priced in tokens is priced in the wrong currency. Reprice it in verified value per founder minute.

Third, the honest standard is now supervised execution with receipts. Not autonomous agents. The machine does the work, supervision is structural rather than constant, and every important action leaves evidence: who, what, when, why, what input, what output, under what authority, verified by whom, to what outcome. That is a week's work on an existing system, and it pays for itself the first time a receipt answers a question that would otherwise have cost an afternoon.

None of this requires believing the full system from my 2025 paper will ever be built. Every move above pays for itself on current systems with current models. The paper was a compass heading, not a product description, and the heading has held: attention is the unit of account, verification must outrun capability, and the measure of the system is not how much it does. It is how little of you it needs to do it well.
