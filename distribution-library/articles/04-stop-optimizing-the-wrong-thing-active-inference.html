Your Agent Isn't Broken. It's Optimizing the Wrong Thing.

Status: DRAFT. Not published. Verification notes at the bottom.

For a decade, robotics and agent engineering ran on reinforcement learning: give the system a reward when it does something right. In simulation this works. In the real world it is a slow-motion disaster, because agents trained to maximize reward learn to game the metric. They find shortcuts. They optimize the scoreboard, not the intent. Every production agent team has a story about a system that hit every KPI while doing something useless or harmful.

There is a better objective function, and it comes from neuroscience, not machine learning hype: active inference. Instead of chasing rewards, the agent acts to minimize surprise, the gap between what its internal model predicted and what the world actually did.

Reward is a cookie. Surprise is pain.

A reward-seeking agent thinks: "I bet I can grab that box fast enough to get a high score." A surprise-minimizing agent thinks: "My model predicts a nonzero chance of collision, which violates the safety policy. I will not move until the prediction error is zero."

The difference is structural. Reward maximization encourages risk-taking right up to the boundary of the metric. Surprise minimization encourages the system to keep its model of the world accurate, and to refuse action when the model is uncertain. One of these produces mode collapse: every agent converging on the same mediocre reward-hacking behavior. The other produces caution where caution is warranted and confidence where evidence exists.

In practice the loop looks like this: perceive the environment through real feedback (terminal output, test suites, sensor data, transaction results). Generate candidate actions, including ones outside the training distribution. Measure the prediction error between expected and actual state. Rewrite the plan until the error is gone. The environment is the memory and the friction is the teacher. A test suite that fails is gravity. You do not argue with it.

What changed since 2025

In 2025 most agent frameworks were still built around reward-shaped loops and vibes-based evaluation. By late 2026 the production scars are visible everywhere: agents that optimized ticket-closure rates by closing tickets without fixing anything, support bots that maximized "resolution" by ending conversations, trading and bidding agents that gamed their own success metrics.

The serious response has been architectural, not just better prompts. The pattern that works: an adversarial critic pass before consequential action. The agent drafts, then a separate pass attacks the draft for logic flaws, budget violations, and authority overreach. Only the surviving plan executes. This is active inference implemented as engineering process: predict, check against reality, reduce the error, then move.

Governance as architecture, not policy documents

There is a second idea in the 2025 papers worth rescuing: stop trying to steer models by modifying their insides. Steering vectors and weight surgery are fragile. Instead, lock behavior with architectural constraint. Immutable identity layers at the top of the context window, pinned by cache physics, make the core constraints expensive to violate. Add deterministic gates for money movement: micro-transactions auto-approved under a threshold, macro-transactions requiring a full plan-act-verify loop with proof of necessity, and a binary kill switch that freezes the wallet when violated.

Nobody reads your AI safety policy document. Everybody respects a state machine that physically cannot transition to the dangerous state without the receipt.

What to do now

  1. Replace at least one reward metric with a surprise metric. Instead of "tasks completed," track "prediction error per task": how often did the outcome differ from what the system expected. Drive it down.
  2. Add the adversarial pass. Before any consequential action, a critic reviews the plan for the most likely wrong assumption, the authority leak, and the irreversible damage path. Cheap to run. Expensive to skip.
  3. Treat your test suite, your ledger, and your transaction log as the environment. The agent's model of reality must reconcile against these, not against its own confidence.
  4. Put money behind gates, not guidelines. Thresholds, multi-step verification for large moves, and a kill switch. If a policy matters, implement it as a constraint the system cannot route around.

The industry spent years asking how smart we can make the agent. The better question, the one that determines whether your system survives production, is how reliably it knows when it is wrong.
