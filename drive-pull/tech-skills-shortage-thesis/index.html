There is a real thesis buried in this, but I would sharpen it because “unions caused the tech-skills shortage” is too easy to knock down.

The stronger argument is:

Middle America built highly effective pipelines for producing skilled industrial labor, but almost no equivalent pipeline for producing skilled technical labor.

For generations, a kid in a small town basically saw two respectable ladders:

College → white-collar profession Apprenticeship / union / trades → electrician, operator, pipefitter, millwright, lineman, etc.

What largely didn't exist was:

high-school → network technician high-school → controls programmer apprenticeship → data-center technician apprenticeship → robotics integrator apprenticeship → cybersecurity operator apprenticeship → industrial AI technician apprenticeship → compute infrastructure specialist apprenticeship → autonomous-systems technician

And culturally, there was another layer:

The trades were masculinity-coded and locally prestigious. Technology was nerd-coded.

That sounds trivial until you realize labor markets are partly cultural machines. A 17-year-old in rural Michigan could see the guy next door making real money as an electrician, equipment operator, welder, or lineman. He probably couldn't see a local 24-year-old earning $90K maintaining GPU clusters or programming industrial controls.

Why data centers expose the problem

Data centers are almost the perfect collision point.

They require:

Traditional skilled labor

electricians pipefitters HVAC construction generators substations mechanical systems

Technical labor

networking Linux storage server hardware controls security telemetry automation eventually robotics and AI infrastructure

America has mature institutions for creating the first category.

For the second category, employers often still write:

“Bachelor’s degree or equivalent experience.”

That's basically an admission that we never built the vocational infrastructure.

So I wouldn't say unions themselves are the villain.

I'd say something more interesting:

The unions proved the model works.

Organized trades figured out decades ago how to:

Recruit → apprentice → train → credential → employ → advance → protect wages → reproduce the workforce.

Tech mostly did:

Go get a degree → teach yourself → somehow get experience → pass six interviews → maybe we'll hire you.

Then Silicon Valley spent twenty years wondering why the country supposedly had a “skills gap.”

That's the contradiction.

And now AI/data-center expansion makes it visible because suddenly America needs hundreds of thousands of people who are simultaneously blue-collar and technical.

Not software engineers sitting in Palo Alto.

People who understand:

power + cooling + networks + compute + sensors + automation + physical systems.

That worker barely has a recognized identity yet.

The opportunity isn't college versus union.

It's creating the missing third pathway:

The Technology Trades

A modern apprenticeship class for AI infrastructure, robotics, autonomous systems, networking, industrial compute, edge systems, drones, sensors, and machine intelligence.

And Middle America may actually be better positioned to produce those workers than Silicon Valley, because the mechanical intuition, troubleshooting culture, physical-world competency, and apprenticeship mentality already exist.

The labor pipeline doesn't need to be invented from zero.

It needs to be redirected one layer upward.

That is a substantially bigger argument than “unions caused the shortage.”

It's:

America industrialized skilled labor but academicized skilled technology. Now the AI buildout is exposing the mistake.

That's a thesis worth building around.

ASSISTANT TURN 2

Yes. The missing center of your argument is that America spent roughly three decades separating “builders” from “technology,” and AI gives us a chance to fuse them back together.

Your thesis is not nostalgia for manufacturing. It is a new industrial model.

The argument underneath what you're saying

From roughly the 1980s into the early 2000s, several things happened at once:

Globalization made imported goods radically cheaper. Software and the internet created enormous wealth, but increasingly concentrated technical opportunity in specific metros and educational pipelines. Small-town manufacturing, independent businesses, repair ecosystems, machine shops, and local ownership weakened. College became culturally positioned as the primary route to the future. Vocational work remained available, but much of it became psychologically disconnected from the technological frontier. A kid who liked computers was told to become a software engineer. A kid who liked machines was told to become a welder, mechanic, electrician, or operator. Almost nobody told them: you can be both.

That division matters.

The kid who might have become the 21st-century equivalent of the garage inventor, radio hobbyist, machinist-engineer, or early computer hacker often got sorted into a predefined lane.

Meanwhile, consumer technology became unbelievably compelling. Instead of computers primarily being things young people built, programmed, modified, and understood, they increasingly became things people consumed through polished interfaces.

The American public became technologically surrounded while often becoming less technically sovereign.

That distinction is important.

You don't need to claim some centralized conspiracy to make the cultural argument. Advertising, entertainment, social media, political messaging, consumer incentives, educational incentives, and corporate incentives can collectively shape behavior without somebody sitting in a room orchestrating the entire thing.

Then AI changes the economics.

Previously, a smart 18-year-old machinist in a town of 8,000 might have an idea for an autonomous vehicle component but face enormous barriers:

CAD knowledge → engineering → electronics → software → simulation → documentation → suppliers → capital → marketing → distribution.

Now AI can compress enormous pieces of that chain.

The machine shop didn't suddenly become obsolete.

It became more powerful.

The welder didn't become obsolete.

The welder potentially gained an engineering department.

The small-town entrepreneur potentially gained:

a software team, industrial-design assistance, CAD guidance, simulation support, research capabilities, procurement assistance, marketing, documentation, coding, electronics guidance, business analysis, and global distribution.

That is the opportunity.

Here is the idea in a more complete form:

Writing The Reindustrialization of the American Imagination

When I graduated high school in 2004, the idea that a kid from a small town could participate directly in building advanced technology still felt foreign.

There were essentially two paths.

You went to college and entered the professional economy, or you learned a trade and entered the physical economy.

Somewhere along the way, America separated the people who understood computers from the people who understood how the physical world actually works.

We shouldn't have.

For decades we enjoyed the upside of globalization, cheap goods, rapid construction, the internet boom, inexpensive electronics, and enormous increases in consumer convenience.

But there was another side to that equation.

Small-town businesses disappeared.

Local manufacturing disappeared.

Machine shops consolidated or closed.

Supply chains moved overseas.

Technical knowledge became increasingly concentrated.

College became the assumed gateway to the future.

And technology increasingly became something Americans consumed rather than something ordinary Americans believed they could build.

The computer revolution created a generation of kids in garages taking computers apart, programming them, building radios, modifying electronics, and imagining completely new industries.

By the 2000s, computers had become dramatically more powerful:but also dramatically easier to consume without understanding.

Then smartphones, social platforms, entertainment systems, advertising algorithms, and increasingly sophisticated political and commercial messaging competed for nearly every waking minute of attention.

We became surrounded by technology while becoming increasingly detached from how technology was actually built.

At the same time, the physical trades continued producing extraordinarily capable people.

Machinists.

Welders.

Electricians.

Mechanics.

Fabricators.

Farmers.

Equipment operators.

Toolmakers.

People who can look at something broken, understand the forces acting on it, and figure out how to make it work.

America still has millions of these people.

What we failed to do was connect that capability to the technological frontier.

AI gives us another chance.

For the first time, the energy required to move from an idea to a functioning product is collapsing.

A machinist in a small Michigan town can now use AI to explore mechanical engineering.

A welder can work through structural concepts.

A farmer can prototype an autonomous implement.

A high-school student can design a robotic system.

A mechanic can experiment with an off-road vehicle platform.

A local entrepreneur can explore battery systems, drones, automation, alternative energy, sensors, rockets, manufacturing equipment, or completely new machines.

AI does not replace the builder.

It gives the builder leverage.

Imagine a high school graduating 135 students.

Today perhaps 35 of those kids already want to work with their hands.

The opportunity is not to convince all 35 to become software engineers.

It is to show them that welding can lead to aerospace.

Machining can lead to robotics.

Electrical work can lead to data centers.

Mechanics can lead to autonomous vehicles.

Farming can lead to agricultural robotics.

Fabrication can lead to defense technology.

Construction can lead to advanced energy infrastructure.

And the kid who loves computers doesn't have to leave town and spend four years learning theory before being allowed to build something real.

Put those kids in the same building.

Give them CNC machines, welders, 3D printers, electronics benches, robotics equipment, compute, AI systems, CAD tools, cameras, sensors, and experienced tradespeople.

Then tell them:

Build something.

Build an electric dirt bike.

Build a rover.

Build a drone.

Build an agricultural robot.

Build a new heating system.

Build a microfactory.

Build a machine that solves a problem for a business down the road.

Build something for the Moon.

Build something for Mars.

Suddenly the cultural equation changes.

Being technically capable becomes cool again.

The machinist becomes part engineer.

The welder becomes part aerospace builder.

The programmer learns how steel behaves.

The farmer becomes an automation designer.

The local manufacturer becomes a technology company.

And the small town stops viewing itself as somewhere young people have to escape in order to participate in the future.

It becomes somewhere the future can actually be built.

That is the opportunity AI creates for Middle America.

Not another generation of people consuming artificial intelligence.

Millions of Americans using artificial intelligence to become inventors, manufacturers, engineers, operators, entrepreneurs, and owners.

America does not have to choose between the industrial economy and the digital economy anymore.

The next great economic opportunity may come from combining them.

AI + skilled trades + local manufacturing + American ingenuity.

We already have the human infrastructure.

We already have the towns.

We already have the shops.

We already have the people who know how to build.

What has been missing is the connective tissue between imagination, engineering, computation, capital, and production.

AI can become that connective tissue.

The objective should not simply be to teach Middle America how to use AI.

It should be to give Middle America the ability to build the future with it.

And there is one concept I'd put at the center of the whole thing:

The AI-enabled tradesman becomes the new garage inventor.

That creates several powerful second-order effects.

Education changes: project-based engineering becomes viable even in a 135-student high school because AI can supplement scarce specialist instructors.

Economic development changes: instead of trying to lure one 800-person factory into town with tax abatements, communities can cultivate dozens of 5-50-person advanced manufacturing businesses.

Career identity changes: a kid doesn't need to choose between “blue collar” and “technology.” The highest-value worker may increasingly be the person who understands both atoms and bits.

Entrepreneurship changes: the minimum viable team required to build a physical technology company shrinks dramatically.

National resilience changes: distributed machine shops, fabricators, technicians, energy workers, farmers, and small manufacturers become part of an adaptable domestic production network.

Culture changes: building becomes aspirational again.

And that last one may be the most important.

You are really arguing for reindustrializing the imagination before reindustrializing the country.

Factories follow people who believe they can build things.

The cultural mission is therefore not merely:

“Learn AI.”

It's:

Stop watching the future happen somewhere else. Build it here.

That gives the whole thesis a much stronger spine.
