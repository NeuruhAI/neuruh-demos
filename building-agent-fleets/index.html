Building Agent Fleets

Superintelligence as Orchestration

Superintelligence may be the orchestration of general intelligence. You do not start with the bots. You start with the outcome. A fleet is a coordinated execution architecture, not a pile of chatbots.

This page is built from a single conversation about cooking, houses, and agent fleets. The kitchen and the house are not decoration. They are the working model: the outcome comes first, the environment is built before the work starts, and the orchestration layer is what turns capable parts into a system that can pursue goals at a scale no individual could pursue alone.

The resource-acquisition question list

Before execution, the fleet must be able to answer whether it can acquire each of these:

How to know a resource is good enough

  • Provenance: where did it come from, and can that source be checked again?
  • Freshness: is it current enough for this decision, or stale enough to mislead?
  • Fit: does it match the schema, format, and granularity the worker expects?
  • Completeness: are the required fields present, or will the worker have to guess?
  • Authority: is the source permitted and trusted for this use?
  • Cost: is the acquisition cost (time, compute, money, attention) worth the result?
  • Fallback: if this resource fails validation, is there a substitute or a stop rule?

A resource that fails any of these is not ready. The fleet either repairs it, substitutes it, or stops and escalates. It does not proceed on hope.

Section 4

Environment as a Variable

The same recipe behaves differently in a different environment. The environment changes execution. A fleet that ignores the environment is a recipe that assumes every kitchen is identical.

The rule: do not ask only "does the agent know the recipe?" Ask "what does this environment do to the recipe?" The answer changes the plan, the tooling, and the expected failure modes.

    Section 6

    The House

    A house is a system of systems. So is a fleet.

    Site selection is the first intelligence layer

    Before a foundation is poured, the site decides what is possible. Location, ground, access, and constraints shape everything downstream. In a fleet, choosing the environment, the data sources, and the constraints is the same decision, and it happens first.

    The smartest action can be "Stop. The premise is wrong."

    A good builder refuses a bad site. A good orchestrator refuses a bad mission. Stopping is not failure. It is the intelligence layer doing its job before resources are spent on a premise that cannot hold.

    A house is a system of systems

    Foundation, structure, plumbing, electrical, envelope, and finish are separate systems that must integrate. A fleet is the same: ingestion, reasoning, tools, memory, governance, and delivery are separate systems, and the integration is the product.

    Scale changes implementation, not first principles

    The $30,000 house and the $3 million house share architecture: site, foundation, structure, systems, envelope. What changes is materials, capacity, and finish. A fleet scales the same way. The architecture holds; the implementation grows.

    The house rule: build the foundation before the finish. A fleet that starts with polish and skips foundations is a house with paint on a cracked slab.

    Section 7

    Scale Explorer: Dinner for 2, 20, or 2,000

    The meal stays a meal. The orchestration problem grows. Pick a scale and see what changes.

    Dinner for 2

    Orchestration: one cook, one plan, direct observation. The cook sees everything, adjusts in real time, and serves when ready.

    What breaks at this scale: almost nothing. The bottleneck is skill, not coordination.

    Fleet equivalent: a single agent with a clear task. Orchestration is trivial because the whole system fits in one head.

    Dinner for 20

    Orchestration: a small team. One person owns the plan, others own stations. Timing matters: dishes must land together. Communication becomes a real job.

    What breaks at this scale: informal coordination. You need a plan, a timeline, assigned stations, and a shared signal for "ready". Without them, some dishes are cold and some are late.

    Fleet equivalent: a small agent team with a sub-orchestrator, shared context, and explicit handoffs. This is where specialization and delegation start to pay.

    Dinner for 2,000

    Orchestration: a catering operation. Procurement, logistics, multiple kitchens, transport, service staff, safety inspection, and a command layer. No single person sees the whole operation. The system must run on protocols, not on one person's attention.

    What breaks at this scale: everything that relied on one person knowing everything. You need redundancy, validation at every handoff, failure recovery, and governance that works without the founder in the room.

    Fleet equivalent: a full agent fleet. Orchestration is the product. The meal is still a meal, but the system that produces it is an entirely different machine.

    Figure 4. Scale explorer. The outcome (a served meal) does not change. The orchestration required to deliver it changes by orders of magnitude.

    Section 11

    Two Articles, One Principle

    Article 1 is the kitchen. Article 2 is the house. They converge on the same principle: build the environment, architecture, dependencies, and orchestration first.

    Article 1: The Kitchen

    The kitchen is the original agent fleet. It runs on resources, knowledge, tools, specialization, preparation, execution, timing, delegation, quality control, scaling, environmental constraints, and dynamic adaptation.

    • Resources: ingredients are data. A cook who cannot source cannot cook.
    • Knowledge: the cookbook is the knowledge base, and the chef's experience is learned domain intelligence.
    • Tools: the knife transforms, the oven executes. The wrong tool is the wrong pan: the work fails at the tool, not at the idea.
    • Specialization: the sous-chef and the specialist cook divide the work by capability, not by convenience.
    • Preparation: mise en place is context engineering. Readiness is built before execution starts.
    • Execution: cooking is processing. Timing and order decide whether parallel work helps or collides.
    • Timing: some steps are sequential, some are parallel. Getting that wrong is a workflow error.
    • Delegation: the chef delegates what others can do better and keeps what only the chef can decide.
    • Quality control: food safety is governance. The taste test is evaluation. Plating is output formatting.
    • Scaling: dinner for 2 and dinner for 2,000 are the same meal and a different operation.
    • Environmental constraints: the kitchen is the operating environment. Change it and the recipe behaves differently.
    • Dynamic adaptation: a missing ingredient is a missing dependency. Substitute, recover, or stop. Do not improvise blindly.

    Article 2: The House

    The house is the fleet as architecture. It runs on requirements, site selection, context, foundations, dependencies, infrastructure, modularity, architecture, specialization, scale, optimization, and structural integrity.

    • Requirements: what must the house do? Requirements come before design, and design comes before building.
    • Site selection: the first intelligence layer. The site constrains everything that follows.
    • Context: the ground, the climate, the access, the neighbors. Context is not decoration. It is a design input.
    • Foundations: the part nobody sees and everything depends on. Skipping foundations is not speed. It is debt.
    • Dependencies: water, power, materials, permits, labor. A missing dependency stops the build, whatever the plan says.
    • Infrastructure: plumbing and wiring are the data and API layers. They must be installed before the walls close.
    • Modularity: rooms are modules. They can be built, inspected, and repaired independently, and they integrate at defined interfaces.
    • Architecture: the plan that makes the systems into a house instead of a pile of materials.
    • Specialization: framers, electricians, plumbers. Each trade owns its system, and the general contractor orchestrates.
    • Scale: the $30,000 house and the $3 million house share architecture. Scale changes implementation, not first principles.
    • Optimization: insulation, layout, and flow are optimized after the structure is sound, not before.
    • Structural integrity: validation. The house must hold under load, weather, and time. So must the fleet.

    Convergence: the kitchen and the house end in the same place. Build the environment, the architecture, the dependencies, and the orchestration first. The agents come last, and they work because everything underneath them already works.
